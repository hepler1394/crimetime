Why Some Cases Stay Cold for Decades

October 2, 2026  ·  Updated October 5, 2026  ·  Cory

The Murder Accountability Project counts more than 345,000 Americans killed since 1965 in homicides that remain unsolved, and says at least 4,000 killers get away with murder every year. Each of those is a case where the investigation ran out of leads. A cold case is an unsolved crime that is no longer under active investigation but where new information could still surface. Most are murders and rapes, crimes that generally carry no statute of limitations, along with disappearances where the person has not been seen for years.

Why one case is solved in a week and another sits for forty years usually comes down to ordinary, practical problems: memories fade, evidence is lost or was never collected, investigators fix on the wrong person, and the tests that might help cost money.

Time works against memory

Eyewitness memory is fallible, and it gets less reliable as months and years pass. Witnesses fill gaps without meaning to. Stress and trauma distort what people recall. Investigators talk about the first hours after a crime or disappearance as the most valuable, because evidence is fresh and memories have not yet been reshaped by news coverage and conversation.

The abduction of Jacob Wetterling shows how long a case can drift. On October 22, 1989, the 11-year-old was abducted while biking home from a convenience store with two other boys in St. Joseph, Minnesota. The FBI questioned a man named Danny Heinrich in December 1989 and released him for lack of evidence. The case stayed unsolved for nearly 27 years. In 2015, new testing of DNA from a 1989 assault on another boy, Jared Scheierl, matched Heinrich. A search of his home led to child pornography charges, and in September 2016, as part of a plea deal on one of those charges, he led investigators to Jacob's remains and admitted in federal court to abducting and killing him.

Evidence and the tools to read it

Physical evidence does not wait. It degrades, gets misplaced, or was never collected to modern standards in the first place. Many cold cases predate the tools that now solve them. The FBI's DNA index, CODIS, began as a pilot program in 1990, and the national index did not go live until October 1998. The FBI's fingerprint system was replaced by the more accurate Next Generation Identification system in 2014.

Even good evidence is useless without something to compare it to. A DNA profile with no match in any database just sits in a box until the database grows or the method changes. The 1976 killing of Karen Klaas in Hermosa Beach, California, is an example. A familial search of the state DNA database in 2011 found nothing. A second search in 2016 matched a close male relative of Kenneth Eugene Troyer, and in January 2017 the Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department named Troyer as her killer. He could never be charged. Police had shot and killed him in 1982.

Tunnel vision

Investigations go wrong in human ways too. Detectives can settle early on a theory or a suspect and stop looking elsewhere. Researchers and courts call this tunnel vision. The cost is two-sided: the real offender is overlooked, and an innocent person can live under suspicion for years.

The Wetterling case had both. Heinrich was questioned within two months and let go. Meanwhile, in 2010, the Stearns County sheriff publicly identified Dan Rassier, a music teacher who lived near the abduction site, as a person of interest, and investigators dug up his farm. Rassier was never charged. After Heinrich's 2016 confession, the sheriff said it essentially cleared him.

Resources shape outcomes as well. High-profile cases draw detectives and money. A killing that never makes the news, handled by a short-staffed department juggling current cases, is at much greater risk of going cold.

When attention does not help

The opposite problem exists too. Elizabeth Short, later called the Black Dahlia, was found murdered in the Leimert Park neighborhood of Los Angeles on January 15, 1947. A total of 750 investigators from the LAPD and other departments worked the case in its early stages, and police interviewed more than 150 men as possible suspects within weeks.

The press coverage was intense and at times obstructive. Reporters from the Los Angeles Examiner called Short's mother in Boston and told her that her daughter had won a beauty contest, to draw out personal details before telling her she had been killed. Detective Sergeant Finis Brown blamed the press for compromising the investigation with its probing and unverified reporting. Over the years more than 500 people have confessed to the killing, some of them born after it happened. It remains unsolved.

How cold cases do get solved

Joe Kennedy, a former North Carolina police officer, retired NCIS special agent in charge and founder of the Carolinas Cold Case Coalition, told USA TODAY that the three most common ways cold cases are solved are physical evidence, witness testimony and confessions. Time, he said, can help as much as hurt. Marriages end, friendships fade, and witnesses who once stayed silent may finally talk. Kennedy said confessions play a larger role than many people realize, because many offenders end up telling someone.

The 1994 killing of Robin Warr Lawrence shows evidence and confession working together. Lawrence, 37, was stabbed to death in her Springfield, Virginia, home on November 20, 1994, while her 2-year-old daughter was in the house. Blood evidence from the scene sat unmatched for decades. In 2023, after Fairfax County cold case detectives sent the DNA to Parabon NanoLabs for genetic genealogy analysis, they identified Stephan Smerk, a former Army soldier living in New York. Within hours of giving a DNA sample, he went to a local police station and confessed. He told them he knew he was going to kill somebody that night but did not know who. Smerk pleaded guilty to first-degree murder in October 2024, and on March 7, 2025, he was sentenced to 70 years in prison.

The cost of new science

Genetic genealogy has opened many old cases, but it is not cheap. Michael Coble, executive director of the Center for Human Identification at the University of North Texas Health Science Center, told USA TODAY that such investigations can cost $8,000 to $10,000, particularly when analysts must first separate a mixture of DNA profiles. For a small department, he said, the choice can come down to a new police cruiser or one case.

David Mittelman, chief executive of the Texas lab Othram, said government funding tends to follow DNA testing protocols that have been in use for years, while newer technology is not subsidized the same way. A federal bill named for Carla Walker, abducted from a Fort Worth bowling alley parking lot in 1974 and found dead three days later, whose case was solved with genetic genealogy nearly 50 years on, would let existing federal grant money pay for genetic genealogy and would require that work to follow Justice Department rules. It passed the Senate in June 2026. As of the most recent reporting found, it was waiting on the House.

For families, the wait has its own name. Psychologists call it ambiguous loss: grief without an ending. The cases that stay cold longest tend to be the ones where several of these problems stacked up at once, and the ones that finally break usually do so because one of them changed.
