How Forensic Genealogy Cracks Cold Cases

July 17, 2026  ·  Updated September 25, 2026  ·  Cory

As of December 2023, investigative genetic genealogy has led to breakthroughs in more than 651 criminal investigations. That number includes the identification of 318 individual perpetrators and 464 previously unidentified decedents. You have probably heard the headline version of this technology. It is the tool that finally caught the Golden State Killer. Here is what the evidence actually shows about how this science works.

A cold case is an unsolved crime that is no longer the subject of a current criminal investigation, but one where new information could still emerge. Typically, these are violent major felony crimes like murder and rape. Unlike minor crimes, these offenses generally do not have a statute of limitations. Sometimes disappearances are also classified as cold cases when a victim has not been seen for some time. The cases of Natalee Holloway and the Beaumont children fall into this category.

Despite advances in forensic science, a substantial number of homicides remain unsolved each year in the United States. This results in a growing backlog of cold cases. Police departments have opened dedicated cold case units to re-examine these files. For decades, investigators hit the same wall. Now, the combination of crime scene DNA and consumer genealogy research is changing the process. Let's unpack it.

The limits of traditional DNA

Traditional forensic DNA testing relies on a straightforward comparison. Investigators collect a biological sample from a crime scene. This can be blood, semen, saliva, hair roots, or tissue. A forensic laboratory then develops a DNA profile from that evidence.

That profile is run against law enforcement databases like the Combined DNA Index System, known as CODIS. CODIS uses a small number of genetic markers called short tandem repeats. The system looks for an exact match. If the perpetrator’s DNA is already in the system, a match emerges and investigators have a lead.

If the DNA is not in the system, the case stalls. DNA evidence is of no value unless there is evidence on file to compare it to. This limitation allowed violent offenders to evade capture for decades. Forensic genetic genealogy bypasses that limitation entirely. It does not require the suspect’s own DNA to be on file. Instead, the process works through relatives.

Building the reverse family tree

The genetic genealogy process begins with extracting a specific type of profile. A genetic genealogist extracts a single nucleotide polymorphism profile, or SNP profile, from the crime scene sample. This profile contains hundreds of thousands of genetic markers. This is far more data than the markers used for CODIS.

The SNP profile is then uploaded to public genealogy databases. Platforms like GEDmatch and FamilyTreeDNA contain the voluntarily submitted DNA data of millions of people. These are individuals who tested their ancestry through companies like 23andMe and AncestryDNA. The system scans for any user whose DNA shares significant segments with the uploaded profile.

The search returns a list of genetic matches ranked by the amount of shared DNA. The method does not require a direct match. In most forensic cases, the closest matches are second, third, or fourth cousins. These are people who share a set of great-grandparents or great-great-grandparents with the unknown individual. From there, the genealogist constructs family trees backward through generations using public records like birth certificates, census data, and obituaries.

The goal is to find where the family trees of multiple genetic matches converge on a common ancestor. From that common ancestor, the genealogist works forward through descendants. They apply filters based on age, sex, geography, and the timeline of the crime. Once a prime suspect is identified, investigators obtain a fresh DNA sample. This is often collected from discarded items like a coffee cup or cigarette butt. A traditional STR analysis confirms the match.

The Golden State Killer breakthrough

The case that launched this field into public consciousness involves a predator who terrorized California. Between 1974 and 1986, this unknown suspect committed at least 13 murders, more than 50 sexual assaults, and over 100 burglaries across multiple jurisdictions. Investigators gave him different names in different regions. He was known as the Visalia Ransacker, the East Area Rapist, and the Original Night Stalker.

DNA linked the crime sprees in 2001, but the profile generated no hits in CODIS. The case went cold again. In 2018, investigator Paul Holes and attorney Anne Marie Schubert enlisted genetic genealogist Barbara Rae-Venter. She uploaded a SNP profile from a crime scene DNA sample to GEDmatch. She identified distant relatives of the unknown offender and traced multiple family lines.

The research pointed to Joseph James DeAngelo, a 72-year-old former police officer living in Citrus Heights, California. Investigators collected DNA from a discarded item in DeAngelo’s trash. It matched the crime scene evidence. DeAngelo was arrested on April 24, 2018. In June 2020, he pleaded guilty to 13 counts of first-degree murder and 13 counts of kidnapping. He received multiple consecutive life sentences without parole.

The murder of April Tinsley

The technology also cracked one of Indiana's most infamous cold cases. On April 1, 1988, eight-year-old April Marie Tinsley was playing with two friends in Fort Wayne. She went back to retrieve her umbrella and disappeared around 3:00 pm. Her mother reported her missing when she did not arrive home for dinner. A jogger found Tinsley's body on April 4, 1988, in a ditch just west of Spencerville, Indiana. Her autopsy report suggested she had been raped and strangled to death.

Between 1990 and 2004, the killer left a series of anonymous messages and notes in the Fort Wayne area. On May 21, 1990, police found a message written in crayon on a barn. It read, "I kill 8 year old April Marie Tisley," and "did you find the other shoe haha I will kill again."

During the Memorial Day weekend in 2004, four more notes were found. Three were left on girls' bicycles, and one in a mailbox. Three notes were placed in plastic bags alongside used condoms and Polaroid pictures of a man's lower body. One note threatened that the reader was his next victim. The DNA from the condoms matched the police's DNA profile of the suspect.

The arrest of John D. Miller

For decades, the case file remained open. The FBI's Behavioral Analysis Unit created a profile of the suspect in 2009. In May 2018, a Fort Wayne Police Department detective sent a sample of the suspect's DNA to Parabon NanoLabs. The company used GEDmatch to identify the suspect's relatives.

On July 2, 2018, genealogist CeCe Moore narrowed the list of suspects to two brothers. One was 59-year-old John D. Miller of Grabill, Indiana. Police found used condoms in Miller's trash and collected DNA that matched the crime scene profile. Detectives approached Miller at his house on July 15, 2018. When asked if he knew why they wanted to talk, Miller replied, "April Tinsley."

Miller confessed to the murder during an interview at the police station. He admitted he abducted Tinsley, raped her, and choked her to death in his trailer. On December 7, 2018, Miller changed his plea to guilty. He was sentenced to 80 years in prison. Miller died of natural causes in a hospital on September 4, 2025.

Naming the unidentified

Forensic genealogy is not just used to find suspects. It is also used to identify unknown victims. In the Bear Brook murders in New Hampshire, the remains of four people were found in barrels in 1985 and 2000. Forensic genetic genealogy helped identify the victims and their killer.

In another historic case, a child found murdered in 1957 remained unidentified for 65 years. In 2022, researchers used DNA testing and genealogy techniques to finally discover his name: Joseph Augustus Zarelli. The technology gives names back to victims who stayed unidentified for years.

In Madison County, Ohio, a man’s skeletal remains were discovered in a farm field in 1989. Over 35 years, environmental factors degraded the DNA. The lab Othram Inc. used specialized extraction techniques and whole-genome sequencing to build a profile. Law enforcement used genealogical research to identify the man as Robert Earl Sanders of Columbus. Giving a name to the nameless re-opens the investigation into the circumstances of a death.

The privacy debate

The rise of forensic genetic genealogy brings significant ethical considerations. The process involves searching the DNA of millions of users who uploaded their data to consumer sites for personal reasons. This raises questions about genetic privacy and consent.

Many people worry that law enforcement might use their genetic data without direct approval. Numerous individuals uploading their DNA are unaware that law enforcement may investigate it. False leads can also affect families. Officers might investigate innocent relatives before finding the correct suspect, which can be stressful and invasive.

As the field evolves, policies are being developed to balance investigative power with individual rights. Several states now require warrants for forensic genealogy searches. Experts urge law enforcement to limit these searches to major crimes and recommend clear judicial oversight.

The synergy between DNA technology and genealogical research is dismantling the concept of the unsolvable case. The records show exactly how this science puts names to the nameless. Read the case files. Form your own conclusion.
