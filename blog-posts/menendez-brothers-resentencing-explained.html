The Case File: Erik and Lyle Menendez

March 2, 2025  ·  Updated September 14, 2026  ·  Cory

You've probably heard the headline version of the Menendez brothers. Joseph Lyle Menendez and Erik Galen Menendez were convicted of killing their parents, José and Mary Louise "Kitty" Menendez, in their Beverly Hills home in 1989. The strangest true detail of the case isn't the crime scene itself, but the alibi. On the night of August 20, 1989, Lyle and Erik went to a movie theater and attempted to purchase tickets for the film Batman to prove they were not at home. They abandoned the plan when they realized the theater time-stamped its ticket stubs.

I went back through the case file. What the evidence actually shows is a timeline of documented purchases, a massive spending spree, and a legal battle that has spanned more than three decades. Following the murders, Lyle and Erik claimed that unknown intruders were responsible, suggesting a potential mob killing. Police initially investigated this angle, but the financial records quickly pointed the investigation in a different direction.

Today, the legal status of Lyle and Erik Menendez is shifting. After spending 35 years behind bars, a judge resentenced the brothers in May 2025. Let's unpack it. Here is what the record shows about the murders, the trials, and where the case stands right now.

The Timeline of August 20, 1989

The timeline of the murders begins two days before the killings. On August 18, 1989, Lyle and Erik visited several Southern California gun stores to buy handguns. Due to a two-week waiting period mandated by gun laws and issues with Lyle's driver's license, they opted for shotguns instead. They purchased Mossberg shotguns and ammunition at a Big 5 Sporting Goods store in San Diego. Erik used a driver's license stolen from Lyle's friend, Donovan Goodreau, to make the purchase.

On the evening of August 20, José and Kitty were watching the 1977 film The Spy Who Loved Me in the theater den of their Beverly Hills mansion. Lyle and Erik entered carrying the loaded shotguns. José was shot six times, including a fatal shot to the back of his head. Kitty was shot ten times. The record shows she was on the floor crawling away before the fatal shot. Lyle ran to the car where Erik handed him ammunition to reload before firing the final shot.

After the killings, the brothers left to dispose of their blood-stained clothes and buried the shotguns along Mulholland Drive. They headed to the "Taste of L.A." festival at the Santa Monica Civic Auditorium. Upon returning home, Lyle called 9-1-1 and told the operator, "Someone killed my parents!" Police officers and forensic staff described the scene as brutal. Retired police detective Dan Stewart stated, "I've seen a lot of homicides, but nothing quite that brutal." The autopsy report noted that one blast caused "explosive decapitation with evisceration of the brain" to José.

The Spending Spree

Detectives initially investigated Lyle's suggestion that the murders were a result of mob-related activity, given José's business connections as the CEO of Live Entertainment. However, in the months after the killings, Lyle and Erik spent hundreds of thousands of dollars. The brothers left the Beverly Hills mansion unoccupied and moved into adjoining condominiums in Marina del Rey.

The financial records document exactly where the money went. Lyle bought Chuck's Spring Street Café, a Buffalo wing restaurant in Princeton, New Jersey, along with three Rolex watches and a Porsche Carrera sportscar. Erik hired a full-time tennis coach and competed in overseas tournaments. They took trips to the Caribbean and London. During this time, they spent approximately $700,000. Most of this money came from a $650,000 personal life insurance policy that was paid out.

This spending caused police to view the brothers as suspects with a financial motive. Family members disputed this connection, asserting there were no changes in their spending habits. But police also heard from Glenn Stevens, a friend of Lyle's. Stevens reported that one week after the killings, Lyle made a sudden trip to Princeton to erase a new will José had prepared. A computer expert hired by Lyle confirmed he was paid to ensure the deletion of this will.

The Confession Tapes and Arrests

The investigation gained momentum through a high school classmate of Erik's named Craig Cignarelli. Erik and Cignarelli had co-written an amateur screenplay titled Friends, about a wealthy young man who commits the "perfect murder" by killing his parents for their inheritance. Cignarelli contacted police to report the screenplay and stated that Erik had confessed to him. Police arranged for Cignarelli to wear a wire during a lunch with Erik, but Erik denied the murders on the recording.

Erik eventually confessed to his psychologist, Jerome Oziel. Oziel then told his mistress, Judalon Smyth, and began recording his therapy sessions with Lyle and Erik. After a tumultuous breakup with Oziel, Smyth informed police about the brothers' involvement and the existence of the confession tapes.

Lyle was arrested on March 8, 1990, outside the Beverly Hills mansion. Erik turned himself in three days later, on March 11, after returning to Los Angeles from a tennis tournament in Israel. Both were held without bail and jailed separately at the Los Angeles County Jail. After two years of legal disputes over doctor-patient privilege, the Supreme Court of California ruled in August 1992 that most of the tapes were admissible.

The First Trial and Mistrial

Lyle and Erik's first trial began in July 1993. The trial was broadcast on Court TV, with Leslie Abramson representing Erik and Jill Lansing representing Lyle. The defense argued that the brothers killed their parents out of fear for their lives after a lifetime of sexual, emotional, and physical abuse by José. Erik testified that two weeks before the killings, he first told Lyle about the abuse, leading to family confrontations. They claimed they purchased the shotguns for protection.

Prosecutor Pam Bozanich argued there was no evidence of imminent danger. The prosecution used the taped conversations with Oziel, where the brothers said they planned the murders "beforehand." Jurors were told that mention of sexual abuse was absent in the Oziel tapes and in Erik's earlier confession to Cignarelli. Bozanich played Lyle's staged 9-1-1 call to demonstrate his ability to act and deceive.

The defense presented testimony from the brothers' cousin, Diane Vander Molen, who stated Lyle confided in her about the abuse in 1976. Another cousin, Andy Cano, alleged Erik told him about the abuse when they were children. Despite these testimonies, the juries remained deadlocked. Female jurors suggested voluntary manslaughter, while male jurors pushed for first-degree murder. The trial ended in a mistrial in January 1994.

The Second Trial and Conviction

Los Angeles County District Attorney Gil Garcetti opted to retry the brothers. The second trial began in 1995. Judge Stanley Weisberg did not allow cameras in the courtroom, citing the risk of exposing jurors to outside commentary. Weisberg also limited the defense to sixty-four total witnesses. Erik testified over fifteen days, but Lyle did not testify in the retrial.

Prosecutor David Conn challenged Erik's allegations, asking why he did not join the United States Army to escape his father. Conn also presented a letter allegedly written by Lyle to his ex-girlfriend Traci Baker, which included instructions on how to testify. Expert witness Park Elliot Dietz argued for the prosecution that Erik did not show symptoms of "learned helplessness" because he had purchased guns and practiced at a shooting range.

Judge Weisberg determined there was insufficient evidence to support the claim that the brothers were in imminent danger. Lyle and Erik Menendez were convicted on two counts of first-degree murder with special circumstances for lying in wait, and conspiracy to murder. On July 2, 1996, they were sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole.

New Evidence and Resentencing

Beginning in 1998, the brothers filed numerous legal appeals, which were repeatedly rejected by state and federal courts. The case shifted in May 2023, when the brothers requested a new hearing based on a habeas corpus petition. The petition cited a 1988 letter Erik allegedly wrote to his cousin, Andy Cano, detailing the abuse. It also included allegations from former Menudo member Roy Rosselló, who stated on NBC's Today that he was drugged and raped by José Menendez when he was 14.

On October 24, 2024, Los Angeles district attorney George Gascón announced he was recommending a resentencing of the case, citing the length of time served, their behavior in prison, and the dysfunction in the Menendez home. Newly elected district attorney Nathan Hochman opposed this petition, calling the self-defense claims "self-serving lies" and arguing the 1988 letter was not credible.

Despite the opposition, a judge resentenced the Menendez brothers in May 2025 to 50 years to life. Because they were under twenty-six years old when they committed the murders, this new sentence made them immediately eligible for parole under youth offender laws.

Where the Case Stands Today

At their first parole hearings in August 2025, both Lyle and Erik Menendez were denied release by the California Board of Parole Hearings. In separate hearings, commissioners cited ongoing rule violations and concerns about public safety. Lyle's denial specifically referenced repeated cell phone infractions while incarcerated.

The brothers are currently scheduled for an early second parole suitability hearing in March 2027. They also have a potential path to release through clemency, which could be granted by California Governor Gavin Newsom in the form of a commutation or a full pardon.

The 1989 murders in Beverly Hills left a massive paper trail of receipts, court transcripts, and recorded tapes. The legal system has debated the motive for 35 years, and the upcoming 2027 hearing will write the next chapter. Read the file. Form your own conclusion.
