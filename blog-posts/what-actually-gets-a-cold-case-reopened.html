How Cold Case Units Decide Which Files to Reopen

August 14, 2026  ·  Updated September 25, 2026  ·  Cory

The strangest true detail about cold cases is that the police do not just give up on them by definition. The National Institute of Justice defines a cold case simply. It is any case where the probative investigative leads have been exhausted. That phrasing matters. A case does not move again because of an anniversary or because someone feels strongly about it. It moves when a new lead exists.

The statistics paint a bleak picture. An estimated 242,000 unresolved homicides occurred in the United States from 1980 to 2016. Overall, roughly 40 percent of the homicides in the United States are unsolved. The clearance rate for homicides plunged from 78.3 percent in 1975 to 59.4 percent in 2016.

Reopening a case usually starts small. It is an inventory of what sits in evidence storage and a decision about what is worth sending back to the lab. Let's unpack it.

Funding the science

A case often reopens because of physical evidence that was never tested, or that was tested with methods that are now out of date. Biological samples once thought unsuitable have later yielded usable DNA profiles. A swab from a steering wheel or a cigarette butt can carry a profile that the lab of 1995 could not read but the lab of today can.

Funding shapes which of those samples actually get retested. Lab work is not free. The National Institute of Justice has run a competitive grant program called Solving Cold Cases with DNA since July 2004. In 2005, it distributed $14.2 million across 38 agencies. By 2007, it had granted more than $8 million to another 21 agencies.

The money pays for personnel, laboratory equipment, investigative travel, and outsourcing samples to private labs. A small police department with a handful of unsolved homicides usually cannot absorb that cost on its own. A grant award can be the practical event that brings a stack of old files back to a working desk.

The Rasmussen file goes cold

You can see exactly how this works in the murder of Sherri Rasmussen. On February 24, 1986, Rasmussen was found dead in the Van Nuys condominium she shared with her husband, John Ruetten. She had been beaten and shot three times. The Los Angeles Police Department initially considered the case a botched burglary.

Lead detective Lyle Mayer and his partner Steve Hooks investigated. The crime had atypical elements for a burglary, like a bite mark on Rasmussen's arm. Hooks found the bite mark unusual, as bites during struggles are much more commonly inflicted by women, while the majority of burglars are men. However, the burglary theory stood, and the investigation stalled after several months.

Rasmussen's father, Nels, believed that LAPD officer Stephanie Lazarus was a prime suspect. Lazarus had formerly been in a relationship with Ruetten. Nels told the police about her, but Mayer dismissed it, telling him there was nothing there. The case went cold for over two decades.

A missing swab

In the late 1990s, the LAPD formed a new unit to look through forensic evidence from cold case files. They wanted to determine whether any had the potential for new leads through DNA testing. In 2004, criminalist Jennifer Francis was able to analyze evidence from the Rasmussen residence.

Francis turned to the bite swab. The detective she was working for had noted it was missing from the property log. Calls to the county coroner's office initially found it missing there, too. The criminalist who originally collected it explained that samples that old had not been logged into the computer database. It was eventually found after searching the lab's freezers.

Francis did not find any matches in the FBI's Combined DNA Index System, known as CODIS. But she did find that the saliva in the swab had come from a female. This undermined the initial burglary theory. When Francis asked the supervising detective if the female LAPD officer had been investigated, he insisted the case was simply a burglary. The evidence went back into the files.

Fresh eyes on the evidence

In March 2008, Van Nuys homicide detective Jim Nuttall found a box with two case files left on the floor near his desk. One was the Rasmussen killing. He and his partner Pete Barba agreed it was not likely a burglary because the DNA test pointed to a female suspect. They looked at the case as a murder, with the burglary staged to throw the police off the trail.

The case file showed aspects of the crime that were implausible for a break-in. Rasmussen's jewelry box was in plain view and untouched. The condo was in the middle of a gated complex. The front door had not been forced open. A stack of stereo equipment at the base of the stairs had a thumb-shaped bloodstain on a record player, but no print, suggesting whoever left it wore gloves. The blood was Rasmussen's, suggesting the equipment had been stacked after the struggle.

The detectives developed a list of five female suspects. They eventually eliminated all but Lazarus, who by then was a detective in the Commercial Crimes Division. Realizing Lazarus was their prime suspect, they arranged to discreetly collect a voluntarily discarded DNA sample from her. While off-duty, Lazarus discarded a cup at a Simi Valley Costco. A sample taken from it matched the DNA from the bite mark.

The arrest of an officer

In June 2009, Robbery-Homicide Division detectives Greg Stearns and Dan Jaramillo called Lazarus from the lockup at Parker Center. They claimed they had someone in custody who wanted to talk about an art theft. After she checked in her gun and entered the interrogation room, they explained they were actually trying to tie up loose ends in the Rasmussen case.

Lazarus claimed to recall little but gradually revealed more knowledge until she accused her colleagues of considering her a suspect. The detectives asked if she would provide a DNA sample. She stated she might be prepared to, but said she had to speak with somebody and left the room, where she was arrested.

When the trial began in early 2012, prosecutors argued her motive was jealousy. Prosecutor Paul Nunez told the jury it was not a fair fight, calling it prey caught in a cage with a predator. In March, the jury convicted Lazarus of first-degree murder. She was sentenced to 27 years to life in prison. During a 2023 parole hearing, Lazarus confessed to the crime.

Recognizing a staged scene

Staged burglaries are a recurring theme when cold case units look back at old files. You see similar amateur staging in the 2002 Richthofen case in São Paulo, Brazil. On October 31, 2002, Manfred and Marísia von Richthofen were beaten to death in their mansion by brothers Daniel and Cristian Cravinhos, at the instruction of the couple's daughter, Suzane.

The trio attempted to fake a robbery. They opened the couple's safe, scattered jewels on the floor, and left a revolver intact next to Manfred's body. They cut open a briefcase to fake the theft of cash.

Alexandre Paulino Boto was the first police officer to arrive. He classified the murder as an amateur crime. He noted that if someone wanted to rob the house, they would not leave jewelry, cell phones, and a gun in the bedroom. A thief would not leave a gun on the floor. That kind of staging falls apart when investigators look at what the evidence actually shows. Suzane and Daniel Cravinhos were sentenced to 39 years and 6 months in prison, and Cristian was sentenced to 38 years and 6 months.

The reality of clearance rates

Solving these older cases affects how we understand modern crime statistics. The national homicide clearance rate has fallen over the long term. In 2024, about 61.4 percent of homicides were cleared, up from a record low of around 52 percent in 2022.

One detail in how the FBI counts these clearances matters for cold cases. A case is recorded as cleared in the year it is solved, not the year the crime happened. An arrest made in 2026 for a 1985 killing counts toward the 2026 figure. A department that clears a batch of old cases can lift its current rate even while recent cases stay open.

Most reopened cases do not end in an arrest. A reopening is a sequence of conditions lining up. A piece of evidence still exists, a method exists to read it, money exists to pay for the test, and a database turns the result into a name. Take away any one of those, and the case stays cold.

The reason cold case units keep producing results is that each year, more old evidence becomes testable and more profiles sit in databases waiting to match. Cases that were genuinely out of leads can quietly acquire a new one. Read the file. Form your own conclusion.
