How Podcasts Have Helped Reopen Cold Cases

November 27, 2026  ·  Cory

James Ford Seale was supposed to be dead. He had been arrested in November 1964 in the kidnapping of 2 young men in Mississippi, and the charges were dismissed in January 1965. Investigators came to believe he had died. Then a Canadian journalist and a victim's brother started asking questions and found out he was still alive.

That is what people usually mean when they say a podcast reopened a cold case. Sometimes the claim holds up. Sometimes the podcast arrived after the real break. Often the truth sits in between, and the details matter.

Here is what the record shows for several of the cases most often cited, and where the credit belongs.

What a podcast can actually contribute

A homicide or missing-person investigation has stages. Someone has to generate a lead, check whether it's reliable, find physical evidence, identify a suspect, and then establish guilt through a lawful process. A podcast can help with one stage without touching the others.

It might prompt a witness to call police. It might push an agency to reexamine its files. It might lay out a chronology investigators later find useful. Those are real contributions. But claiming too much credit can bury the work of detectives, forensic specialists and witnesses, and online theories can expose private people to harassment before there's any evidence against them.

The fair test is simple. Ask what changed after the series aired, what officials have confirmed, and what is still unanswered.

Henry Dee and Charles Moore: Someone Knows Something

On May 2, 1964, 19-year-old Henry Dee and Charles Moore were hitchhiking near Meadville, Mississippi, when a car pulled up and the men inside demanded they get in. Klan members took them to the Homochitto National Forest, beat them, drove them to Louisiana, weighed them down and left them to drown in the Mississippi River. Several months later, a team searching for 3 missing civil rights workers found their bodies.

The FBI arrested Klansmen Charles Marcus Edwards and James Ford Seale in November 1964. The district attorney dismissed the charges in January 1965. Many local officers refused to talk because of their ties to the Klan, and Seale was thought to have died.

David Ridgen of the Canadian Broadcasting Corporation came across the case while researching another story. He spent 10 months finding Thomas Moore, Charles's brother, and in 2005 the 2 men traveled to Mississippi together. That work became Season 3 of Someone Knows Something, along with the documentary Mississippi Cold Case. They learned Seale was alive and used that to persuade officials to reopen the case.

Edwards was granted immunity in 2006. In 2007, Seale was convicted of conspiracy and kidnapping and sentenced to 3 life terms.

Lyn Dawson: The Teacher's Pet

On January 9, 1982, 33-year-old Lyn Dawson disappeared from her home in Sydney, Australia, leaving 2 daughters and her husband, physical education teacher Chris Dawson. He said she had left the family for a religious cult. On the day she vanished, Joanne Curtis, a student of his, moved into the home. Police later concluded she had likely been murdered but couldn't recover a body or gather enough evidence to arrest anyone.

In May 2018, journalist Hedley Thomas launched The Teacher's Pet for The Australian. The series examined the couple's tumultuous marriage, Chris Dawson's relationship with his student, and the shortcomings of the original police investigation.

On December 4, 2018, police arrested Chris Dawson for Lyn's murder. New South Wales police commissioner Mick Fuller said "additional evidence," including 2 pieces unearthed by the podcast, "helped [them] tie the pieces of the puzzle together." The record here ends with that arrest, so he is described only as accused.

Tara Grinstead: Up and Vanished

On October 22, 2005, Tara Grinstead, a 30-year-old high school history teacher in Ocilla, Georgia, didn't show up for work. Her door was locked, her car was outside, and her keys and purse were gone. The one real clue was a single latex glove in the front yard, with a fingerprint nobody could match. In 2010, a judge declared her legally dead.

Atlanta filmmaker Payne Lindsey read the Georgia Bureau of Investigation's online cold case file and posted his thoughts on a forum. A private investigator who had spent 10 years on the case asked for his help. Up and Vanished debuted in 2016, and as it went on, locals grew more willing to talk to him.

About 6 months after the podcast began circulating, police arrested Ryan Duke, a former student of Grinstead's, and a few weeks later Bo Dukes. According to 48 Hours, Dukes's girlfriend reported his involvement to the Georgia Bureau of Investigation. Authorities thanked the public for keeping the investigation alive but didn't name the podcast. The court outcomes are more complicated than any one-line claim that a podcast caught the killer. Grinstead's body has never been found.

Kristin Smart: Your Own Backyard

Kristin Smart disappeared in California in 1996. Decades later, Chris Lambert launched Your Own Backyard, revisiting the case through interviews and records and organizing details many listeners had never seen in one place.

Arrests came in 2021. In 2022, Paul Flores was convicted of Smart's murder. His father, Ruben Flores, was found not guilty on an accessory charge.

The San Luis Obispo Tribune reported that law enforcement and the Smart family credited Lambert with bringing witnesses and attention to the case. The conviction itself rested on courtroom evidence. Kristin Smart's body has not been recovered.

Karen Schepers: when detectives host the show

The Elgin Police Department in Illinois created its own podcast, Somebody Knows Something, in which detectives discussed unsolved cases and asked the public for information. The first season focused on Karen Schepers, who disappeared in 1983 after a night out with coworkers.

In 2025, investigators found her car in the Fox River with her remains inside. Chicago Magazine reported that tips arising around the podcast told investigators about the bar and possible routes, though no caller told them where to search. The podcast opened a channel, and detective work did the rest. It answered where she was, not every question about why the car entered the water.

Mike Williams: the case podcasts reached late

On December 16, 2000, Mike Williams left for a duck-hunting trip on Lake Seminole and never came home. His boat was found, his body wasn't, and officials floated the idea that alligators had taken him, even though alligators don't generally feed in winter. He was declared legally dead, and his wife, Denise Williams, received $1.5 million in insurance. His mother, Cheryl Williams, ran ads, put up billboards, pushed the Florida Department of Law Enforcement to reopen the case in 2004, and later wrote over 200 letters to Governor Rick Scott that were forwarded unopened to the FDLE.

One of her ads drew Tallahassee Democrat reporter Jennifer Portman, who wrote a long story in 2006. An episode of Disappeared followed in 2011. The break came from Brian Winchester, a longtime friend of Williams who later married Denise. He was arrested in 2016 for kidnapping her at gunpoint and sentenced in December 2017 to 20 years. The next day, the FDLE announced Williams's remains had been found. At Denise's trial, Winchester testified that he shot Williams at her direction. Her defense noted he hadn't been charged despite that admission.

Denise Williams was convicted in December 2018. In November 2020, an appeals court overturned her murder conviction but upheld her conspiracy conviction and its 30-year sentence. As of September 2025, she was imprisoned at the Florida Women's Reception Center. The podcasts came later: Crime Junkie in early 2019, Over My Dead Body in August 2023, Plot House in October 2024. A mother and a newspaper reporter kept this case alive.

Line these cases up and you get a range, from reporting that put a suspect back in front of officials to coverage that came after the answers. Check the timelines and the official statements, and judge for yourself how much each podcast moved its case.
