The Documented Facts of the JonBenét Ramsey Case

January 8, 2026  ·  Updated September 14, 2026  ·  Cory

The ransom note demanded exactly $118,000. John Bennett Ramsey pointed out to the first police on the scene that this amount was nearly identical to his Christmas bonus from the prior year. It is one of the strangest true details of the case, stated plainly in the record. I went back through the case file on the killing of six-year-old JonBenét Patricia Ramsey to see what the evidence actually shows. On the night of December 25, 1996, JonBenét was killed in her family's home at 755 15th Street in Boulder, Colorado. She was reported missing early on December 26, and her body was found about seven hours later in the basement of the house.

The autopsy determined that the cause of death was asphyxia by strangulation associated with craniocerebral trauma. Her skull had an 8.5 inch fracture on the right side of her head, and a garrote was tied around her neck. The case was ruled a homicide. JonBenét was born on August 6, 1990, in Atlanta, Georgia. She was the younger of two children of Patricia "Patsy" Ramsey and John Ramsey. During the 1996-97 school year, she was enrolled in kindergarten at High Peaks Elementary School in Boulder. Patsy had entered JonBenét in various child beauty pageants, where she won titles like America's Royale Miss and Little Miss Colorado.

The only people known to be in the house on the night of JonBenét's death were her immediate family: Patsy, John, and their nine-year-old son Burke. The ransom note contained specific instructions against contacting police and friends, but Patsy telephoned the police at 5:52 a.m. MST. She also called family and friends. Two police officers responded to the 9-1-1 call and arrived at the Ramsey home within three minutes. Let's unpack exactly how the investigation unfolded from that moment, based strictly on the documented facts.

The First Hours and the Search

When the first officers arrived, they conducted a cursory search of the house but did not find any sign of forced entry. Officer Rick French went to the basement and came to a door that was secured by an additional wooden latch at the top of the door frame. He paused for a moment in front of the door, but walked away without opening it. French later explained that he was looking for an exit route used by the kidnapper. Since the wooden latch was holding the door closed from inside the house, he reasoned the kidnapper could not have used this door and then closed the latch from the inside. JonBenét's body was later found behind that exact door.

With JonBenét still missing, a forensics team was dispatched to the house. The team initially believed that the child had been kidnapped. Because of this, JonBenét's bedroom was the only room in the house that was cordoned off to prevent contamination of evidence. No precautions were taken to prevent contamination in the rest of the house. Friends, victim advocates, and the Ramsey family's minister arrived at the home to show support. Visitors picked up and cleaned surfaces in the kitchen, possibly destroying evidence.

Boulder detective Linda Arndt arrived at about 8:00 a.m. MST, in anticipation of receiving further instructions from the kidnapper. However, there was never an attempt by anyone to claim the money. Meanwhile, neighbor Scott Gibbons claimed that around midnight, he saw the upper kitchen of the Ramsey residence lit up with dimmed lights. Another neighbor, Melody Stanton, reported that she awoke shortly after midnight to the sound of a child's scream coming from the Ramsey residence.

Discovery of the Body and Autopsy

At 1:00 p.m. MST, Detective Arndt asked John Ramsey and Fleet White, a family friend, to search the house to see if anything seemed amiss. They started their search in the basement. John opened the latched door which Officer French had overlooked, and found his daughter's body lying in one of the rooms. JonBenét's mouth was covered with duct tape, a nylon cord was found around her wrists and neck, and her torso was covered by a white blanket. John picked up her body and rushed upstairs. When JonBenét was moved, the crime scene was further contaminated, and critical forensic evidence was disturbed for the returning forensics team.

The autopsy revealed a garrote made from a length of nylon cord and the broken handle of a paintbrush. Part of the bristle end of the paintbrush was found in a tub containing Patsy's art supplies, but the bottom third of it was never found despite extensive searching. There was no evidence of conventional rape, although sexual assault could not be ruled out. No semen was found, but there was evidence of a vaginal injury, and the pathologist recorded that it appeared her vaginal area had been wiped with a cloth.

The autopsy also revealed a vegetable or fruit material which may represent pineapple, which JonBenét had eaten a few hours before her death. Photographs of the home taken on the day her body was found show a bowl of pineapple on the kitchen table with a spoon in it. Neither John nor Patsy said they remembered putting the bowl on the table or feeding pineapple to JonBenét. Police reported that they found Patsy Ramsey's fingerprints on the bowl.

The Ransom Note

Patsy Ramsey reported that she found a two-and-a-half-page handwritten ransom note on their kitchen staircase. The Federal Bureau of Investigation told the police that it was very unusual for such a note to be written at the crime scene. The note and a practice draft were written with a pen and notepad from the Ramsey home. The note appears to echo film dialogue, with films like Ruthless People, Ransom, Escape from New York, Speed, and Dirty Harry considered to be potential sources.

The police believed that the note was staged because it did not have any fingerprints except for Patsy's and authorities who had handled it. According to a Colorado Bureau of Investigation report, there were indications that the author of the ransom note was Patricia Ramsey, though the evidence fell short of a definitive conclusion. A federal court later ruled it highly unlikely that Patsy wrote the note, citing six certified handwriting experts.

Investigators looked at several theories behind the $118,000 demanded. They considered employees at Access Graphics who might have known the amount of John's prior bonus. They also considered the possibility that the ransom demand was a reference to Psalm 118 and spoke to religious sources to determine possible relevance. The amount remains a gripping detail that anchors much of the speculation surrounding the initial hours of the investigation.

The Family-Member Theory

Boulder police initially focused almost exclusively on John and Patsy Ramsey. The police saw no evidence of a forced entry, but they did see evidence of staging of the scene. In 1999, a grand jury returned a true bill to charge the Ramseys with placing the child at risk in a way that led to her death. However, Boulder County District Attorney Alex Hunter did not prosecute them. He stated there was insufficient evidence to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

In 1998, Boulder Police Chief Mark Beckner said Burke Ramsey was not involved in the killing of his sister, and the District Attorney's office reiterated in 1999 that Burke was not a suspect. Despite this, a 2016 CBS documentary miniseries featured a group of experts who theorized that Burke hit his sister in the head with a heavy object after she stole a piece of pineapple from his bowl. On behalf of Burke Ramsey, his counsel filed defamation lawsuits against CBS. In 2019, attorney L. Lin Wood announced that the lawsuit had been settled to the satisfaction of all parties.

The Ramseys have consistently maintained their innocence. They offered a $100,000 reward in a newspaper ad in April 1997. John Edward Douglas, a former FBI special agent hired by the family, contends that John and Patsy did not kill their daughter, criticizing the media for trying the case in the court of public opinion. Defamation suits were filed by the Ramseys and their friends against several media outlets over the years to combat the intense public scrutiny.

The Intruder Theory and DNA Evidence

The intruder theory is supported by physical evidence found at the scene. On the night JonBenét was killed, there had been two windows left slightly open, a broken basement window, and one unlocked door. Detective Lou Smit concluded that an intruder had committed the crime, pointing to an unidentified boot mark left in the basement room where JonBenét's body was found. Critics of this theory noted an intact cobweb in the broken basement window and undisturbed foliage outside.

In December 2003, forensic investigators extracted enough material from a mixed blood sample found on JonBenét's underwear to establish a DNA profile. That DNA belonged to an unknown male person and excluded the DNA of each of the Ramseys. The sample was submitted to the FBI's Combined DNA Index System, but it did not match any profile in the database. In 2008, Boulder District Attorney Mary Lacy announced that newly developed touch DNA analysis excluded the Ramsey family members as suspects, and she publicly exonerated them.

Subsequent district attorney Stan Garnett stated in 2016 that Lacy's decision was not legally binding. In October 2016, a report said that new forensic analysis with more sensitive techniques revealed that the original DNA contained genetic markers from two individuals other than JonBenét. Former Boulder police chief Mark Beckner has stated that the unknown DNA from JonBenét's clothing has got to be the focus of the investigation.

In 2006, John Mark Karr, a 41-year-old school teacher, was arrested in Thailand following a false confession to murdering JonBenét. Karr claimed to have drugged her, but the autopsy indicated no drugs were found in her body. Authorities did not find any evidence linking Karr to the crime scene, and his DNA did not match the DNA found on JonBenét's body.

Where the Case Stands

In 2009, the Boulder Police Department resumed control of the investigation. In October 2010, the police reopened the cold case, conducting new interviews following a fresh inquiry by a committee that included state and federal investigators. The Boulder Police Department continues to treat the case as an open homicide and provides periodic public updates. Detectives have followed up on more than 21,016 tips, letters, and emails, and traveled to 19 states to interview more than 1,000 individuals.

The killing of JonBenét Ramsey drew extensive national and international media attention, fueled by her pageant participation, the family's wealth, and the unusual evidence. The Boulder Police Department continues to work with DNA experts and law enforcement partners across the country. The facts of this case are complex, the evidence is heavily debated, and the killer remains unidentified. Read the file. Form your own conclusion.
