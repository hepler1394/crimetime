Investigative Genetic Genealogy

January 20, 2025  ·  Updated September 14, 2026  ·  Cory

As of December 2023, a single investigative technique has solved 651 criminal cases. That number includes 318 individual perpetrators and 464 decedents who were finally identified. You have probably heard the headline version of this process, known as investigative genetic genealogy. It is the tool that caught Joseph DeAngelo, the Golden State Killer.

Here is what the evidence actually shows about how this works. Investigators take crime scene DNA and upload it to publicly accessible genealogy databases like GEDMatch and Family TreeDNA. They are not looking for a direct match to the killer. They are looking for the killer's relatives. By finding identity-by-descent segments of DNA that indicate shared ancestors, genealogists build massive family trees.

They use demographic identifiers like age, gender, and place of residence to narrow down the branches. Let's unpack it. Here is how genetic data from consumer testing companies is rewriting the rules of cold case investigations.

The Database Difference

The US government operates the Combined DNA Index System, or CODIS. It contains genetic profiles of approximately 18 million different people. But CODIS is limited. It only holds DNA samples from convicted felons and arrestees. If a perpetrator has never been arrested, their DNA will sit in CODIS without a match forever.

Investigative genetic genealogy bypasses that limitation. Public databases like GEDMatch contain genetic profiles from everyday people who took a test to learn about their ancestry. GEDMatch has roughly 1.2 million profiles. That relatively small number is incredibly powerful. Data shows it is capable of identifying a third cousin or closer in over 90 percent of the population.

There is a distinct demographic difference between the two systems. Data suggests that 8.6 percent of the entire African American population is present in the CODIS database, compared to only 2 percent of the white population. Meanwhile, genetic profiles from direct-to-consumer databases and GEDMatch consist of 75 percent white individuals from Northern European descent.

The Angie Dodge Investigation

To understand the power and the complications of this technology, I went back through the case file for the murder of Angie Dodge. The crime occurred in Idaho Falls, Idaho, on June 13, 1996. For decades, the investigation was a tangled mess of false leads and coerced confessions.

In January 1997, police questioned a 20-year-old named Chris Tapp. After over 100 hours of intense interrogation, Tapp confessed. No physical evidence tied him to the scene. His confessions were contradictory. Regardless, Tapp was convicted in May 1998 for aiding and abetting the rape and murder, and sentenced to a minimum of 30 years in prison.

Tapp recanted in 2001. In 2007, the Idaho Innocence Project reviewed his interrogation tapes and concluded he had been heavily coerced. Tapp served 20 years in prison before his rape conviction was vacated in 2017. He was fully exonerated of the murder charge in July 2019. He later sued the city of Idaho Falls and reached an $11.4 million settlement in June 2022.

The Wrong Genetic Lead

While Tapp was sitting in prison, authorities continued to search for the person who actually left DNA at the crime scene. In 2014, they turned to a process called Y-chromosome familial searching. This led them to accuse a filmmaker from New Orleans named Michael Usry Jr.

The connection came from Usry's father. Michael Usry Sr. had previously contributed a DNA sample to the Sorenson Molecular Genealogy Foundation for recreational and religious purposes. Ancestry.com later acquired that database. Idaho Falls authorities got a court order to search it. They found a match of 34 out of 35 searched alleles with Usry Sr., indicating a close relative was a match to the crime scene.

Because Usry Jr. had records of trips through Idaho Falls, he became a suspect. Police interrogated him and obtained a saliva sample. A proper DNA test proved he did not match the crime scene evidence. The allegations were dropped. Usry stated that the investigation was a severe breach of his and his father's privacy.

Finding the True Perpetrator

The trail went cold again until late 2018. Idaho Falls authorities made another attempt using investigative genetic genealogy. They brought in CeCe Moore at Parabon NanoLabs. She searched GEDMatch for near-matches to the crime scene DNA.

The results indicated the murderer had to be a descendant of a specific couple: Clarence and Cleo A. Landrum Ussery. Moore identified six known male descendants. One lived in Idaho in 1996. Police tailed him, collected his DNA, and it was not a match.

After three more months of research, Moore found a seventh, previously unknown suspect descended from that same couple. His name was Brian Leigh Dripps Sr. He had lived in Idaho Falls in 1996, right across the street from the Dodge household. Police collected a DNA sample from a cigarette butt thrown from his car window. It was a complete genetic match. Dripps confessed, pled guilty in February 2021, and was given a life sentence.

Decades of Cold Cases Cracked

The Dodge case is just one example. Investigative genetic genealogy has systematically dismantled the concept of the unsolvable case. Look at the record of serial offenders identified through these methods. Roy Charles Waller, known as the NorCal Rapist, committed violent home invasions from 1991 to 2006. In September 2018, genetic genealogy linked him to the crimes. A jury convicted him in 2020 on 46 counts.

In Indiana, Steven Ray Hessler committed a series of violent home invasions and sexual assaults in the early 1980s. Parabon Nanolabs identified Hessler as the prime suspect decades later. Investigators confirmed the match using DNA collected from his utility bill envelope. He was convicted on all charges in March 2022.

The technology is also being used in active investigations. Matthew J. Nilo, a 35-year-old attorney, was arrested in May 2023. He is accused of a series of cold case sexual assaults in Boston between 2007 and 2008. Authorities used forensic genetic genealogy to connect him to the victims. He has pleaded not guilty to the charges.

Giving Names to the Unidentified

This process is not just for finding suspects. It is used to identify unknown victims. In Madison County, Ohio, a man’s remains were discovered in a farm field in 1989. For 35 years, he was a John Doe. Using whole-genome sequencing and genetic genealogy, authorities identified him as Robert Earl Sanders.

The DNA Doe Project, a non-profit organization, has been instrumental in resolving these cases. They helped identify 15-year-old Cheryl Lynn Edwards. Her body was found in the Mississippi River in 1975. A team of 16 volunteer genealogists worked the case over a weekend, identified her grandparents, and gave her back her name.

In November 2000, 16-year-old Tiffany Bradley vanished. Her remains were found in Chelsea, Massachusetts, and she was known as Chelsea Jane Doe for 26 years. In 2026, the FBI used a DNA profile built by Othram to put together a family tree. They matched her with her brother.

The Privacy Debate

The rapid expansion of this tool has initiated a serious debate over the Fourth Amendment. Direct-to-consumer companies hold highly sensitive information about medical traits and familial associations. 23andMe states in its privacy policy that they will not provide information to law enforcement without a valid court order, subpoena, or search warrant.

Public databases are different. The US Supreme Court established the third-party doctrine, which states a person has no legitimate expectation of privacy for information voluntarily turned over to third parties. When users upload their profiles to GEDMatch, they forfeit their privacy to the data. However, following the arrest of the Golden State Killer, GEDMatch changed its terms of service. They now require individuals to opt into the use of their profiles by law enforcement.

The rules are still being written. The world's first Investigative Genetic Genealogy Center launched at Ramapo College of New Jersey in December 2022 to train practitioners. The technology is powerful, but it relies entirely on the data of everyday citizens. Read the file. Form your own conclusion.
