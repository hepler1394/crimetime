The Trial and Appeals of Adnan Syed

November 24, 2026  ·  Cory

Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts

In 1999, high school student Hae Min Lee vanished in Baltimore County and was found dead weeks later in Leakin Park. The investigation centered on her ex-boyfriend, Adnan Syed, and the trial that followed went on to hold the country's attention. The episode walks through the disappearance, the cell tower evidence, and the testimony that sent Syed to prison, then the appeals, the vacated conviction, and its reinstatement, which have kept the case in the courts for more than twenty years.

This transcript is the episode script as written and fact-checked, timed to the recording. Timestamps are approximate within a sentence.

0:05 What's up guys, welcome back to CrimeTimeSnacks. In December 1999, a murder trial ended in a mistrial because jurors overheard a sidebar dispute between the defense attorney and the presiding judge. The attorney interpreted a statement by the judge as tantamount to accusing her of lying, and she said as much. She was unaware that members of the jury were within earshot. The defendant was Adnan Syed. He was tried again, found guilty of the murder of Hae Min Lee, and sentenced to life in prison plus 30 years.

0:31 For years, it seemed like an open-and-shut case. Today he remains convicted of first-degree murder. No court has found him innocent. And he is not in prison. Let's unpack how all three of those can be true. Hae Min Lee was born in South Korea on October 15, 1980. In 1992 she emigrated to the United States with her mother, Youn Kim, and her brother, Young Lee, to live with her grandparents. Her mother said she moved to America from Korea to give her kids a better life. Lee attended the magnet program at Woodlawn High School in Baltimore County, Maryland.

1:01 She played lacrosse and field hockey, and she managed the boys' wrestling team. She was popular at school. She had dreams of being an optician, and she had a job at LensCrafters. In 1998 she dated a classmate named Adnan Syed. According to court records, their relationship was kept a secret due to religious and cultural differences, and they broke up in December of that year. Lee wrote about him in her journal. "He's the cutest, sweetest, and coolest guy," she wrote, "and he loves me."

1:27 Biography.com reports that the secrecy eventually frustrated Lee, and that this was what purportedly drove a wedge between them. After they broke up, Lee began to date a man named Don Clinedinst, who worked with her at LensCrafters. Adnan Masud Syed was born in Baltimore on May 21, 1981. His parents are Shamim and Syed Rahman, and Biography.com describes them as conservative Muslim parents. He is the middle child of three sons. Tanveer is his older brother, and Yusuf is his younger brother.

1:55 At Woodlawn High School, Syed was popular and a straight-A student. He was the homecoming king. He played on the varsity football team, and he worked part-time for a paramedic service. He and Lee were both seniors when she disappeared. On January 13, 1999, fellow students last saw Hae Min Lee at her high school around 2:15 p.m. The Baltimore Sun reported at the time that she was driving her 1998 Nissan Sentra, on her way to pick up her 6-year-old cousin and then head to her job.

2:20 She never showed up to either. Her family reported her missing that day, after she failed to pick up her cousin from daycare. Police officers called various friends of Lee to try to find her. They reached Adnan Syed around 6:30 p.m. He told them he had last seen her around the end of classes. At 1:30 a.m., police reached her then-current boyfriend, who said he had arrived home at 7 p.m. What happened on that day depends on who you talk to. As Syed described it to Serial, he went to his photography and English classes, then called his friend Jay Wilds and offered to lend him his car during his lunch period.

2:52 Wilds then dropped him back off at school. Syed had a free period, and then he arrived late to his psychology class. It started at 12:50 p.m. His teacher noted that he entered at 1:27 p.m. After class, by his account, he went to the Woodlawn Public Library to check his email and chatted with a friend, Asia McClain. Then he went to track practice. Wilds picked him up, and the two went to a friend's house. It was while he was there that Officer Scott Adcock called, asking if he knew where Lee was.

3:20 According to the 2019 appeal, Syed told the officer that he was supposed to get a ride home from her, but he got detained at school and felt that she just got tired of waiting and left. Serial reported that Syed then picked up food on his way to meet his dad at the mosque for evening prayers. On February 6, a dog-led search was conducted around Woodlawn High School. On February 9, Lee's partially buried body was discovered in Leakin Park in Baltimore. It was nearly four weeks after she went missing.

3:48 The man who found her was a maintenance worker named Alonzo Sellers, known as Mr. S on Serial. On HBO's docuseries about the case, he described pulling over. He said, "I had to go to the bathroom so I pulled over, and I went further in the woods so no one could see me." He went on. "When I looked down, I seen something that looked like hair, something was covered by the dirt. And I looked real good again, and that's when I seen what looked like a foot." Her autopsy revealed that she had been killed by manual strangulation.

4:13 She was 18 years old. On February 12, the Baltimore City Police Homicide Division received an anonymous phone call suggesting that investigators focus on Syed. The Undisclosed podcast reported that a Baltimore City police detective, Darryl Massey, received two anonymous phone calls telling him to look into Lee's ex-boyfriend as a possible suspect. CrimeStoppers was offering a $3,075 reward for information on the case leading to an indictment. On February 16, Baltimore Police applied for cellular-phone records for a phone belonging to Syed.

4:42 One of Syed's friends was Jay Wilds. Wilds told the police that Syed had expressed intentions of killing her. He stated that he had helped bury the body after Syed confessed to the killing on January 13. Syed was arrested on February 28, 1999. According to Biography.com's timeline, Lee's car was found around 4:30 a.m., and around 6 a.m. Syed was arrested at his home on the 7000 block of Johnnycake Road. He was 17 at the time, but he was charged with first-degree murder as an adult.

5:11 Lee's brother, Young Lee, was 16. Speaking for his grandparents and his mom, he told The Baltimore Sun the news provided, quote, "some closure and some peace." He also said, "We were kind of surprised because she told us that he was one of her best friends." In March 1999, Asia McClain wrote two letters to Syed. She said she remembered talking with him at the Woodlawn Public Library on the afternoon Lee disappeared, potentially during the window when prosecutors said the murder took place.

5:36 Syed's family hired defense attorney Cristina Gutierrez to represent him. His first trial began the second week of December, and it quickly ended the way you heard at the top. His second trial began in January and lasted six weeks. The state's case rested on two things. One was the testimony of Jay Wilds. The other was cell phone records, which prosecutors used to put him near the burial site around 7:00 p.m. Biography.com notes that prosecutors could not offer any physical evidence against Syed.

6:03 On the stand at the second trial, Wilds said the two of them drove to Security Square Mall. He claimed Syed said he was going to kill Lee. He said that in the afternoon, Syed asked him to pick him up at Best Buy, where Syed showed him Lee's body in the trunk of her car. Wilds said they left the car at a park-and-ride lot. That night, he said, they buried her in Leakin Park and left her car in a residential parking lot. According to Wilds, Syed was angry that Lee had broken up with him.

6:28 Prosecutors built their timeline around an incoming call at 2:36 p.m. They argued it was Wilds getting a "come and get me" call from Syed at Best Buy. An AT&T radio frequency engineer, Abe Waranowitz, testified about what the tower connections meant for Syed's location. Biography.com says the tower records corroborated some of Wilds' timeline of events. The state also had a corroborating witness, Jennifer Pusateri. She claimed Wilds told her that Syed confessed to Lee's murder and had shown him the body.

6:56 Wilds was the only person who directly implicated Syed. His account shifted across multiple police interviews and again at trial. It shifted on where Lee was killed, where he first saw her body, when Syed allegedly told him the plan, how many shovels were used, and whether they visited Patapsco State Park that day. It was not contested at trial that Wilds had lied in every pretrial statement to police. The jury credited him anyway. Wilds pleaded guilty to being an accessory to the murder and was given a five-year suspended sentence.

7:25 Biography.com reports that since the conviction, Wilds has changed his story multiple times. It says recent analysis of his police interviews suggests he was heavily coached by the Baltimore police. On February 25, 2000, after a two-hour deliberation, the jury found Syed guilty of first-degree murder, kidnapping, false imprisonment and robbery. The Baltimore Sun reported that he remained calm as handcuffs were being put on him. He said, "I have faith in the Lord. I know I didn't kill her.

7:51 The Lord knows I didn't kill her." Lee's mother, Youn Wha Kim, spoke too. She said, "I would like to forgive Adnan Syed, but as of now, I just don't know how I could." And she said, "When I die, my daughter will die with me. As long as I live, my daughter is buried in my heart." The paper reported that she almost collapsed as she stepped off the witness stand and was escorted out, and that her sobs from outside the courtroom could still be heard inside. Judge Wanda K. Heard gave Syed a life sentence plus 30 years.

8:18 His family fired Gutierrez immediately following the verdict. Now the cell phone evidence. The two Leakin Park calls the state used to place Syed at the burial site were both incoming calls. AT&T's own records included a fax cover sheet. It stated that outgoing calls only were reliable for location, and that incoming calls would not be considered reliable location information. That disclaimer was never shown to the defense. It was never shown to Waranowitz either. Years later, Waranowitz submitted an affidavit.

8:48 He said that if he had known about the disclaimer, he would not have confirmed the phone's location based on incoming calls without first understanding the technical reasons behind the warning. Then there is Asia McClain. Gutierrez never contacted her. Serial reported that after Syed was convicted, a family friend and attorney named Rabia Chaudry asked McClain to write an affidavit. Syed first appealed his case in 2003, to no avail. In May 2010 he sought post-conviction relief, alleging ineffective assistance of counsel.

9:15 He argued that Gutierrez had failed to investigate McClain as an alibi witness. McClain maintained she was talking with Syed in the library at the exact time that prosecutors said he attacked Lee in a Best Buy parking lot several miles away. His appeals lawyer also brought into consideration the unreliability of the cell phone tower records from the original trial. Hearings were held in the Circuit Court between November 2010 and October 2012. In January 2014, the petition was denied by Martin Welch.

9:42 He found that Gutierrez's failure to call McClain as a witness was strategic rather than an act of incompetence, because McClain's timeline was not consistent with Syed's stated timeline. From October 3 to December 18, 2014, her murder and the arrest and trial of Adnan Syed was the subject of the first season of the podcast Serial. It was developed by the creators of This American Life and hosted by Sarah Koenig. Rabia Chaudry had brought the story to Koenig. In the podcast, Koenig talks to Syed, who says he is innocent.

10:10 The episodes generated international interest in the trial. They were downloaded more than 100 million times by June 2016. Biography.com says the podcast placed doubt on the guilty verdict, and that the media coverage opened up new avenues for Syed and his defense team. After Serial ended, there were discussions by the Innocence Project about conducting DNA testing of the physical evidence collected in 1999. In 2015, Chaudry and two other attorneys, Susan Simpson and Collin Miller, began producing a podcast called Undisclosed.

10:39 Chaudry said she is Syed's friend from childhood and strongly believes in his innocence. Simpson and Miller became interested in the case from listening to Serial. Simpson persuaded Waranowitz, the cell phone expert, to sign an affidavit stating that his original testimony was incorrect. Investigation Discovery aired a one-hour special called Adnan Syed: Innocent or Guilty? on June 14, 2016. Its findings were based on a new analysis of evidence brought up in the podcasts. Two books about the case were published that year.

11:09 Confessions of a Serial Alibi, written by Asia McClain Chapman, was released on June 7. Adnan's Story, written by Rabia Chaudry, was released on August 9. In a February 2016 statement, Lee's family said they remained convinced of Syed's guilt. They added that it was now, quote, "more clear than ever" that he killed their daughter. After the 2014 denial, Syed filed a motion seeking leave to appeal in the Court of Special Appeals. The court granted that motion and sent the case back to the circuit court, instructing it to consider reopening his application in light of an affidavit filed by McClain.

11:41 He also filed a supplement asking for a reexamination of the cell-tower evidence used in his trial. On November 6, 2015, Judge Welch re-opened the proceedings. The hearing was originally scheduled to last two days. It lasted five, from February 3 to February 9, 2016. It was attended by people from across the United States, including Sarah Koenig. McClain had reaffirmed her account in a 2015 affidavit, and at the hearing she testified to the library conversation. On June 30, 2016, Welch vacated the conviction and granted the request for a new trial.

12:14 He ruled that Gutierrez, quote, "rendered ineffective assistance when she failed to cross-examine the state's expert regarding the reliability of cell tower location evidence." Welch denied the defense team's motion for bail in the interim. Prosecutors and Attorney General Brian Frosh asked the Court of Special Appeals to reverse that ruling. They argued that Syed's defense attorney did a thorough job, and that Asia McClain would not have changed the outcome of the case. On March 29, 2018, the Court of Special Appeals, the second-highest court in Maryland, upheld the new trial.

12:45 Its opinion said Syed's counsel failed to contact a potential alibi witness who could, quote, "have raised a reasonable doubt in the mind of at least one juror." In May 2018, HBO announced it would produce a four-hour documentary called The Case Against Adnan Syed. The first part of the four-part series was released on March 10, 2019. It revealed that in 2018, Syed turned down a plea bargain that would have required him to plead guilty in exchange for a shortened sentence. Subsequently, his mother told him that she had leukemia.

13:14 USA Today wrote that when the series covered the case in 2019, Syed and his loved ones, who strongly believed in his innocence, had little hope that his conviction would be overturned. Aja Romano of Vox argued that the docuseries has a, quote, "pro-Syed bias" and is "armed with an agenda," though its creators billed it as an objective view of the case. The prosecution took the new-trial ruling to the top court in the state, then called the Maryland Court of Appeals. On March 8, 2019, in a split 4 to 3 ruling, it reversed the lower courts and denied his request.

13:45 The majority agreed that Syed's legal counsel was deficient for failing to pursue alibi witnesses. But they found there was not a significant or substantial possibility that the verdict would have been different. They said McClain's account, quote, "does little more than call into question the time that the state claimed Ms. Lee was killed and does nothing to rebut the evidence establishing Mr. Syed's motive and opportunity to kill Ms. Lee." The court also ruled that he had waived his right to re-examine the validity of the cellphone tower evidence, because the issue had not been raised as part of his original petition.

14:16 On November 25, 2019, the Supreme Court of the United States rejected Syed's appeal. Brian Frosh said in a statement, "We remain confident in the verdict that was delivered by the jury and are pleased that justice for Hae Min Lee has been done." Documents obtained by The Baltimore Sun in early 2019 show that Maryland prosecutors tested multiple items tied to the murder in mid-2018. Syed's DNA did not match any of the DNA present. In 2014, Marilyn Mosby was elected Baltimore City State's Attorney on a platform of criminal justice reform, and with a record of scrutinizing past convictions for potential misconduct.

14:51 In April 2021, attorneys for Syed brought his case to the attention of her office's Sentencing Review Unit, a body established to reexamine lengthy sentences imposed on juvenile offenders. The review expanded into a broader reinvestigation of the underlying conviction. On March 10, 2022, the State's Attorney joined a request from the defense lawyer, Erica Suter. It asked the court to order new DNA testing on Lee's clothing, shoes and rape kit. The joint motion stated that those items had never been tested for DNA.

15:20 On March 14, a city judge ordered that the Baltimore police send evidence to a crime lab in Hayward, California, within 15 days. Prosecutors arranged touch DNA testing on items from 1999, including Lee's skirt, pantyhose, shoes and jacket. The shoes carried a DNA mixture from four individuals. Syed's DNA was not found on any item tested. On September 14, 2022, Mosby's office filed a motion to vacate the conviction. It cited evidence the original prosecution had never disclosed, and new DNA results.

15:51 The motion relied on a Supreme Court case, Brady versus Maryland. Under Brady, prosecutors violate due process when they suppress evidence favorable to the accused that is material to guilt or punishment. The motion cited two Brady violations. First, it said the prosecution had suppressed evidence related to other potential suspects. Second, it said the prosecution had failed to disclose the identity of a suspect who had previously threatened Lee's life and had the means, motive and opportunity to kill her.

16:18 The motion detailed that there were two chief alternate suspects. It did not identify them. It said one of them had threatened to kill Lee, and that her car was found parked behind a house in Baltimore that belonged to one of them. It also said one of the two was convicted of serial rape after Syed's trial. The motion also expressed concerns about the reliability of the cell-phone records and the witness testimony used at the trial. It was partially based on a note by a former prosecutor, Kevin Urick.

16:43 A line from that note was quoted in the motion. It read, "He told her that he would make her disappear; he would kill her." Urick said the motion had misinterpreted his note. He said that "he" did not refer to an alternative suspect, but to Syed himself. The State's Attorney's Office released a statement accusing Urick of attempting to "save face" by now attributing the threats to Adnan Syed. It pointed out that the threats had not been used at any of the previous trials. And it emphasized that the note was, quote, "not the only document relied upon by the court to find a Brady violation."

17:14 On September 19, 2022, Circuit Court Judge Melissa Phinn vacated the conviction. Syed was released from prison the same day. He had served twenty-three years. USA Today described what happened next. Syed left the courthouse and returned to his parents' house. A tree that was only the size of a stick in his memory now towered over him. His dad kissed him on the forehead and repeatedly told his son, "I'm so glad to see you." In October 2022, prosecutors announced that the charges had been dropped.

17:42 Serial released a follow-up episode detailing the reasons for his release. Hae Min Lee's brother, Young Lee, appealed the decision. He argued that, as a victim, he had not been given sufficient notice of the hearing, and that he had not been given an adequate opportunity to speak or testify. He argued that the state had violated Article 47 of the Maryland Declaration of Rights, which gives crime victims the right to be notified of, attend, and be heard at criminal justice proceedings.

18:07 The family said they received insufficient notice to attend the September 2022 hearing in person. On March 28, 2023, the appellate court reinstated Syed's conviction. The vote was 2 to 1, and the court stayed the effective date for 60 days. The panel acknowledged that Judge Phinn had given Young Lee the opportunity to address the court during the hearing. But it found that he was owed, and not given, sufficient notice to attend in person. The panel also faulted prosecutors for failing to, quote, "state in detail" the reasons to throw the conviction out.

18:37 It noted that the motion did not identify the two alternate suspects or explain why the State believed those suspects committed the murder without Syed. In a dissenting opinion, Judge Stuart Berger argued that the appeal was moot, since the charges had been dropped, and that Lee's rights had not been violated. The ruling sparked debate over the appropriate scope of victims' rights in innocence cases. In late April 2023, Syed asked for reconsideration. He argued that the ruling rested on an issue of procedure that did not affect the outcome in the lower court.

19:07 The motion was denied, on the ground that the argument had not been raised originally. He took the case to Maryland's highest court, which put the reinstatement on hold while it considered the appeal. On August 30, 2024, the Supreme Court of Maryland affirmed in a 4 to 3 decision. The murder conviction was reinstated. The court held that a victim's right to be heard at a vacatur hearing includes the right to address the merits after the prosecutor and defense have made their presentations.

19:33 It also found error in the circuit court's off-the-record review of evidence the parties never introduced at the open hearing. The case was sent back for a new vacatur hearing before a different judge. Syed remained released from prison pending the outcome. In December 2024, Syed's legal team filed a motion to reduce his life sentence under Maryland's Juvenile Restoration Act. The act was enacted in 2021. It lets people sentenced as adults for crimes committed before age eighteen petition for a reduced sentence after serving at least twenty years.

20:02 Syed was seventeen at the time of the offense. On February 25, 2025, Baltimore State's Attorney Ivan Bates announced that his office was withdrawing the previously filed motion to vacate. But Bates said he supported the request for a reduced sentence. After a lengthy hearing on February 26, Judge Jennifer Schiffer said she had, quote, "very heavy considerations" to weigh. She said she would issue a written decision. On March 6, 2025, Schiffer issued it. She reduced the sentence to time served, with five years of supervised probation.

20:32 She wrote that Syed is, quote, "not a danger to the public and that the interests of justice will be better served by a reduced sentence." Those are the findings the act asks a court to make. The conviction stays on his record, and he remains free. His attorney, Erica Suter, said in a statement, "Adnan is committed to continuing to be a productive member of his community and living a life centered around his family." Biography.com wrote that the new sentence has not cleared his name, but that it effectively marked an end to his fight for freedom.

21:02 In December 2022, Syed began working at Georgetown University as a program associate for the school's Prisons and Justice Initiative. The judge authorized him to travel to Washington, D.C., and Virginia for work without needing prior approval from his probation agent. On September 18, 2025, HBO premiered a follow-up episode of its docuseries. The episode began filming in 2021 and was originally planned for release in 2023. According to that episode, Syed now lives in Virginia with his wife, whose identity is unknown.

21:33 She wrote to him in 2019, while he was in prison. At the time, she had just finished the docuseries about his trial. They have now been married for five years. Rabia Chaudry told USA Today that numerous women wrote to Syed during the release of Serial, and that he really wouldn't respond. Of his wife, Chaudry said, "She was probably the first one he wrote back, in all these years." According to Chaudry, his wife is a Pakistani American woman living in Virginia who watched the HBO docuseries.

22:00 Chaudry said, "She watched it, and she just felt really depressed because at the end of the documentary, they say that the conviction was reinstated, he's not getting out." Chaudry said, "They started communicating through letter and then over phone. And then she came to visit him in prison." At first Chaudry was skeptical. Then she met her. She said, "After I met her, I was like, 'You're a lucky guy. She's lovely.'" Chaudry said Syed is still, quote, "the same kind, considerate, nonconfrontational, positive person he's always been."

22:28 She said, "Now that it's over, I do believe he wants to go to college and finish his degree." Chaudry considers the murder unsolved. She said, "I think people need to still keep asking the questions and understanding that for every innocent person who's in prison, there is a person who is a killer who's out there." According to the docuseries, as of May 2025, the Baltimore City Police Department had yet to test the new DNA results against alternative suspects. After the Supreme Court's decision in 2019, Maryland Attorney General Brian Frosh stated that the evidence linking Syed to Lee's death is overwhelming.

23:01 So here is where it stands. The conviction is a final judgment. What changed is the sentence attached to it. No court has found him innocent. He is not in prison. And he has always maintained his innocence. Biography.com put it this way. Amid all the twists in the extended legal saga, justice for Lee has remained elusive. Read the file.
