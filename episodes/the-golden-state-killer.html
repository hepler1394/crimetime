Hunting the Golden State Killer

September 12, 2026  ·  Cory

Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts

For more than forty years, California was haunted by an elusive predator known across decades by different names: the Visalia Ransacker, the East Area Rapist, the Original Night Stalker, and ultimately, the Golden State Killer. Leaving behind dozens of violated homes, traumatized survivors, and shattered families, the attacker seemed to vanish into thin air by the mid-1980s, evading traditional police work and fingerprint databases alike. In this episode, I walk you through how a predator who once wore a police badge managed to operate in the shadows, and how investigators revolutionized criminal forensics by using genetic genealogy to crack open the coldest trail in California history. We examine the timeline, the evidence, and the long road to unmasking an ordinary retiree living in a quiet suburban neighborhood.

0:05 What's up guys, welcome back to CrimeTimeSnacks. Here is the strangest detail in the case file, stated plainly. For decades, California police hunted three distinct monsters: the Visalia Ransacker, the East Area Rapist, and the Original Night Stalker. They turned out to be one single man, and while he was committing his earliest attacks, he was wearing a police badge. His name is Joseph James DeAngelo Jr. He has admitted to thirteen murders, committed between 1975 and 1986. As part of his plea deal in 2020, he also admitted in open court to dozens of sexual assaults that the law no longer allowed anyone to charge him with.

0:38 He is believed to be the man behind roughly a hundred and twenty burglaries in and around Visalia as well. He is not an accused man. He is a convicted one, serving life without parole, and I am going to talk about him that way. This episode is about two things. The first is how he was finally identified after more than four decades: not through a tip, not through a confession, but through a family tree, built out of the DNA of his distant relatives. The second is what the record says, as investigators and prosecutors later laid it out, and what was said to him in court in August 2020.

1:09 Before any of that, though, he was a young man training to be a cop. Let's unpack it from the beginning. DeAngelo was born on November 8, 1945, in Bath, New York. His father, Joseph Senior, was a sergeant in the United States Army, and for part of his childhood the family lived in West Germany, where his father was stationed. The record says he was regularly beaten by both parents. As a teenager he went to Mills Junior High in Rancho Cordova and then to Folsom High School, where he played junior varsity baseball, and he later earned a high school equivalency certificate in 1964.

1:38 Prosecutors say that in those teenage years he was already committing burglaries and stealing mail, and that he tortured and killed animals. In September 1964 he joined the Navy. He served twenty-two months during the Vietnam War as a damage controlman, aboard a cruiser and then a destroyer tender. Back in California, he studied police science at Sierra College in Rocklin and graduated with honors. He went on to a bachelor's degree in criminal justice at Sacramento State, took more police training at the College of the Sequoias in Visalia, and completed a thirty-two-week internship with the Roseville Police Department.

2:11 On paper, he was qualified for exactly the job he took next. One more piece of his early adult life matters later. In May 1970 he got engaged to a nursing student he had met at Sierra College, Bonnie Jean Colwell. She ended it in 1971, after he became manipulative and abusive. After the breakup he tried to force her to marry him by threatening her with a gun. Hold on to her first name. Years later, police reported that during a 1978 attack, the thirty-seventh in the series, DeAngelo repeatedly said, I hate you, Bonnie.

2:41 In November 1973 he married Sharon Marie Huddle, in a ceremony held in Auburn. By then he already had his first police job. From May 1973 to August 1976, he was an officer in Exeter, a small town not far from Visalia in the Central Valley, and he was assigned to the burglary unit. Visalia is a small town about halfway between Sacramento and Los Angeles. On March 19, 1974, someone broke into a house there and took fifty dollars in coins from a piggy bank. It was the first recorded break-in of a spree that would run for about twenty months.

3:13 On a single night, November 30, 1974, there were twelve separate incidents. The burglar was strange from the start. He rifled through possessions and scattered women's underclothes around the rooms. He took low-value things, single earrings, cuff links, coin jars, Blue Chip Stamps, and he often left banknotes and expensive items sitting in plain sight. He climbed fences and moved along parks, ditches and trails. He pried at several windows before picking one, and he left more than one way out open behind him.

3:40 He set dishes or bottles against doors as warning items, so he would hear anyone coming. He wore gloves. Investigators found no fingerprint evidence. He became known as the Visalia Ransacker. Claude Snelling was forty-five, a journalism professor at the College of the Sequoias. In February 1975 he had chased off a prowler he found under his sixteen-year-old daughter's bedroom window. On September 11, 1975, at around two in the morning, he woke to strange noises. He ran out the open back door and found a man in a ski mask in the carport, trying to take his daughter, Beth.

4:11 The man had subdued her by threatening to stab or shoot her. When the professor confronted him, the intruder shot him twice. Claude Snelling staggered back into the house to his wife, and he died. The attacker punched and kicked Beth, three times in the face according to prosecutors, left her on the ground, and ran. Police later found a stolen bicycle linked to him nearby, on Redwood Street. Joseph DeAngelo was a serving police officer in Exeter that night. Forty-five years later, he would plead guilty to this murder.

4:38 Visalia police put more resources into the case. A four-thousand-dollar reward was posted. Beth Snelling underwent hypnosis to try to recover more details. Police set up nighttime stakeouts near houses the Ransacker had already prowled. The break-ins kept coming anyway. On December 12, 1975, at about eight-thirty at night, a masked man came into the back yard of a house in a neighborhood the Ransacker had been reported to frequent. Detective William McGowen was waiting inside the garage.

5:04 When he tried to detain the man and fired a warning shot, the man shrieked, pulled off his mask and pretended to surrender. Then he jumped a fence, pulled a revolver with his left hand, and fired once near McGowen's face, shattering the detective's flashlight. Other officers rushed in, and he got away anyway. He left tennis shoe tracks and dropped loot behind him, Blue Chip Stamps and a sock full of coins. McGowen said later he had seen the man before. The likely reason is in the record.

5:29 As an Exeter officer, DeAngelo had helped in the search for the Ransacker. He had been looking for himself. After the December shooting, the Ransacker's burglaries in Visalia stopped. In August 1976, DeAngelo left Exeter for the police department in Auburn, about a thirty-minute drive from Sacramento. He would keep that badge until 1979. And the same year he moved, 1976, a new series of attacks began in the unincorporated suburbs east of Sacramento: Carmichael, Citrus Heights, Rancho Cordova.

5:58 On June 18, 1976, a man broke into the home of a woman who lived alone in Rancho Cordova and sexually assaulted her at knifepoint. Over the next three years there would be at least forty-nine more attacks like it, spreading out of Sacramento County to Stockton, Modesto and Contra Costa County, and as far as Danville, just outside San Francisco. The press called the attacker the East Area Rapist. He chose middle-class neighborhoods and single-story houses that sat near a school, a creek, a trail or open ground, somewhere he could leave fast.

6:27 Most victims had seen or heard a prowler before they were attacked, and many had already had break-ins. Police came to believe he sometimes went into a home ahead of time to unlock windows, unload any guns he found, and leave ligatures where he could reach them later. He phoned future victims too, sometimes for months beforehand, learning their daily routines. At first he went after women alone or with their children. Then he started choosing couples, and investigators believe that change was a direct response to news reports saying he only attacked women who were alone.

6:55 He came in through a window or a sliding glass door and woke the couple with a flashlight in their eyes and a handgun. He made the woman tie up the man before he bound her. He stacked dishes on the man's back and said that if he heard them rattle, he would kill everyone in the house. Then he took the woman to another room and assaulted her, often more than once. He could stay for hours, going through drawers, eating in the kitchen, drinking beer. Victims sometimes thought he had left, and then he would jump out of the darkness again.

7:19 One survivor who has spoken publicly is Kris Pedretti. On December 18, 1976, she was fifteen, home alone while her parents were at a Christmas party, playing the piano at six in the evening. He put a knife to her throat and said, Do what I say or I'll kill you and be gone in the dark. Michelle McNamara later took the end of that sentence as the title of her book on the case. Kris Pedretti has also described what came after: the intimate questions police asked a fifteen-year-old, and the evidence exam that followed.

7:44 She called those the second and third victimizations of that night. When neighbors or patrols spotted him, he went over fences or rode off on a bicycle. Once, he shot and seriously wounded a young man who chased him. He was seen a number of times, and he always got away. He also talked. On March 18, 1977, the Sacramento County Sheriff's Office took three calls from a man claiming to be the attacker. In the third he said, I'm the East Side Rapist and I have my next victim already stalked and you guys can't catch me.

8:11 None of those calls were recorded. That December he got bolder. On the ninth, a woman he had attacked answered her phone and heard a hoarse whisper: Merry Christmas. It's me again. She told investigators she would never forget his voice. The next night, Sacramento authorities got two identical calls, and both were recorded: I am going to hit tonight. Watt Avenue. Patrols were stepped up. At two-thirty in the morning, a masked man on a bicycle was seen on the Watt Avenue bridge, and he slipped past the officers.

8:39 Spotted again two hours later, he dropped the bike and escaped on foot. Around the same time, someone claiming to be the rapist sent a poem called Excitement's Crave to the Sacramento Bee, the mayor's office, and a television station. Then, on January 6, 1978, a man who said he was the East Area Rapist called a counseling service and said, I have a problem. I need help because I don't want to do this anymore. After a short conversation he said, I believe you are tracing this call, and he hung up.

9:05 Less than a month later, on the night of February 2, 1978, Brian and Katie Maggiore were walking their dog in Rancho Cordova, close to where five of these attacks had happened. Brian was twenty-one, a soldier stationed at Mather Air Force Base. Katie was twenty. There was a confrontation in the street, and they ran. Prosecutors say DeAngelo shot Brian, and that Katie ran and yelled for help before he caught up with her and shot her in the head. A shoelace was found nearby. For decades this was only a suspicion.

9:33 In 2016 the FBI said it was confident the East Area Rapist had killed them, and in 2020 DeAngelo pleaded guilty to both murders. In December 1978, after the forty-second attack, in Danville, investigators found three sheets of notebook paper near where a suspicious car had been parked. One was a homework essay about General George Armstrong Custer. One was a journal-style entry about sixth grade, about a teacher who made the writer copy out sentences for hours until his hand ached, and how mad it made him.

10:00 The writer said that anger would scar his memory for life. The third was a hand-drawn map of a neighborhood nobody could identify, with the word punishment written across the back. Detective Larry Pool believed the map was a fantasy, the place the attacker wished he could strike. All this time, the agencies chasing him were barely talking to each other. Sharing was territorial; one deputy said the Sacramento Sheriff's Department wanted the glory of catching him, and egos got in the way.

10:25 By September 1976 that department had linked four attacks, but it asked the media to hold off reporting it until October. In May 1977, Detective McGowen, the man shot at in Visalia, went to Sacramento to see whether this was the same offender. By then there had been twenty-three attacks. After the Maggiore murders, the Sacramento sheriff's office flatly denied any link, and accused the Visalia investigators of looking for publicity. The northern attacks went on into the summer of 1979.

10:52 In July 1979, Auburn police officer Joseph DeAngelo was arrested for shoplifting a hammer and a can of dog repellent. He fought store security and pretended to have a heart attack. When deputies arrived, he rolled around on the chair he was tied to, acting out madness, and then admitted it was an act. He got six months of probation, and that October, Auburn fired him. The same month he lost his badge, the attacks reappeared hundreds of miles to the south. On October 1, 1979, an intruder broke into a home in Goleta, near Santa Barbara, and tied up the couple who lived there.

11:22 They were alarmed to hear him say, I'll kill 'em, to himself, and when he left the room they tried to get out, and the woman screamed. He fled on a bicycle. A neighbor who happened to be an FBI agent chased him, and he dropped the bicycle and a knife and escaped through backyards on foot. That couple were the only people attacked in the southern series who survived. On December 30, 1979, two doctors were found shot to death in a condominium in Goleta: Robert Offerman, a forty-four-year-old orthopedic surgeon, and Debra Manning, a thirty-five-year-old clinical psychologist.

11:53 Offerman's bindings had been untied, which suggested he had lunged at the attacker. The killer had eaten leftover Christmas turkey from the refrigerator. On March 13, 1980, in Ventura, Charlene Smith, a thirty-three-year-old interior designer, and her husband Lyman, a forty-three-year-old attorney, were killed in their home. They were beaten with a log taken from the woodpile beside the house. Three days later, Lyman's twelve-year-old son came by to mow the lawn and found them, and the bedroom alarm clock was still going off.

12:20 Charlene's wrists had been tied with an unusual diamond knot, a knot also seen in the northern attacks, and for a while the killer was called the Diamond Knot Killer. Charlene had been sexually assaulted, and the evidence collected from her would matter, decades later, more than anyone could have known. On August 19, 1980, in a gated community in Dana Point, Keith and Patrice Harrington were beaten to death in their home. Keith was twenty-four, a fourth-year medical student at UC Irvine, on course to graduate early.

12:46 Patrice was twenty-seven, a pediatric nurse. They had been married three months. Keith's brother, Bruce Harrington, would later spend nearly two million dollars supporting California's Proposition 69, which authorized collecting DNA from every felon in the state. In February 1981, in Irvine, twenty-eight-year-old Manuela Witthuhn was killed in her home. Her husband, David, was in the hospital with an illness, so she was alone. The killer kept calling the house afterward. Police and neighbors suspected David, and he lived under that suspicion for years, until DNA testing cleared him.

13:18 He died in 2008. On July 27, 1981, back in Goleta, a few blocks from the Offerman condominium, Cheri Domingo, thirty-five, and Gregory Sanchez, twenty-seven, were killed in a house Domingo was staying in while it was up for sale. The killer came in through a small bathroom window. Sanchez was shot and beaten. Paul Holes, an investigator we will hear a lot more about, later speculated that fighting with Sanchez, a significantly larger man, may have frightened the killer and deterred him from more murders.

13:46 Only one more was ever attributed to him. On May 5, 1986, eighteen-year-old Janelle Cruz was killed in her family's home in Irvine while her family was on vacation in Mexico. The house was for sale, and a realtor who came by to show it found her. A pipe wrench her stepfather reported missing was thought to be the weapon. After Janelle Cruz, there were no more crimes attributed to him. He went dormant. In Santa Barbara County he was known simply as the Night Stalker. When Richard Ramirez was given that name in Los Angeles, the southern killer was renamed the Original Night Stalker.

14:16 And the agencies failed each other again. After the Offerman and Manning murders, Sergeant Jim Bevins, who headed the Sacramento task force, believed it was the same man and called the Santa Barbara sheriff's office, which denied the murders outright. Larry Crompton of the Contra Costa County task force tried next and got the same answer. He learned about them three months later. When Santa Barbara did try to link its couple murders together, Ventura and Orange County authorities were not convinced.

14:41 For most of those years, the best a lab could do with biological evidence was blood typing. Typing could exclude a suspect, but it could never point to one. Then came DNA, and even that took time. In 1997, Paul Holes, by then a Contra Costa County investigator, heard Crompton's theory that the Goleta murders belonged to the northern series. Santa Barbara told him its cases were unrelated, but steered him toward the Irvine police, who were doing something with DNA. Holes' lab used older technology than Irvine's, so the two results could not be compared, and the connection slipped away again.

15:11 In 2001 it finally came together. DNA evidence proved that the East Area Rapist in the north and the Original Night Stalker in the south were the same man. On April 6, 2001, one day after the Sacramento Bee reported the link, one of the women he had attacked in the seventies answered the phone and heard him ask, Remember when we played? That was not the only time he reached back. In October 1982, a woman he had attacked got a threatening call at work, at a Denny's restaurant. Holes believed the caller must have walked in as a customer and recognized her.

15:41 In 1991, another survivor stayed on the line with him for a minute and heard a woman and children in the background. Here is what 2001 could not solve. Investigators now had a DNA profile of the killer, and it went into CODIS, the national criminal DNA database. There was no match. That meant he had never been convicted of a felony that required him to give a sample. A decade later, DNA tied the Domingo and Sanchez murders to him too. The case became one of the reasons California built its own DNA database, which collects samples from everyone accused or convicted of a felony in the state and has been called second only to Virginia's for solving cold cases.

16:17 Still, nobody had a name. In 2013, the writer Michelle McNamara gave him the one that stuck, the Golden State Killer, to tie all of his earlier nicknames together. On June 15, 2016, the FBI and local agencies announced a renewed national effort, with new composite sketches, new details about the crimes, and a fifty-thousand-dollar reward. The work that finally found him started in December 2017, led by Paul Holes and an FBI lawyer named Steve Kramer. It hit a wall almost at once.

16:44 The district attorney at the time refused their request for a DNA sample and told the Irvine police not to release theirs. So they turned to Ventura. The medical examiner there had done something no one else did, preparing a duplicate evidence kit in the Smith case, and it had been frozen since 1980. That sample was pristine. Here is why pristine mattered. CODIS compares about twenty genetic markers, enough to make a unique fingerprint for a match. Genealogy websites work differently.

17:12 They read hundreds of thousands of genetic markers, which together give a rich map of a person's ancestry. The two kinds of profile are incompatible. You cannot turn one into the other, so the old database profile was no help. Investigators needed to build a brand new genealogy profile from the original evidence, and for that they needed high-quality DNA. They uploaded that new profile to GEDmatch, a free public website where people share raw results from consumer DNA testing services.

17:40 DeAngelo himself had never submitted his DNA anywhere. The site turned up ten to twenty people who shared a set of great-great-great-grandparents with the killer. Distant cousins. A team of five investigators, working with the genealogist Barbara Rae-Venter, used that list to start building family trees. In the end they built about twenty-five trees. The one that led to DeAngelo held about a thousand people and reached back more than a century. Over a few months, using clues like age, sex and where people lived, they ruled people out one by one, narrowing almost a million profiles to a single family.

18:14 Two suspects were left. A relative's DNA test eliminated one of them. On March 14, 2018, Joseph DeAngelo was identified as a potential suspect. Paul Holes then called Nick Willick, who had known DeAngelo as an officer in Auburn and later became chief of police there. Willick remembered him as unremarkable, a man who stood too close to people and sulked when he was corrected. Then he told a story. When DeAngelo was being fired, he accused Willick of harassing him and brought a work-stress claim against the city.

18:40 Around that time, Willick's daughter told him a man had shone a flashlight into her bedroom, and Willick dismissed it. As part of his claim, DeAngelo had to see a therapist, and he told the therapist he had gone to Willick's house intending to kill him, but could not find Willick's window. Willick had taken it as a ploy for disability pay. When Holes heard that, he knew they had the right man. They still needed proof that would stand up. On April 18, 2018, investigators quietly swabbed the door handle of DeAngelo's car.

19:07 Later they took a tissue from his curbside garbage can. Both were matched to samples from the Golden State Killer crimes. On the afternoon of April 24, 2018, Sacramento County sheriff's deputies arrested Joseph DeAngelo in the side yard of his home in Citrus Heights. He was seventy-two. He and Sharon had bought that house in 1980, and the family had also kept a second home in Long Beach, where they lived for much of the eighties. They had three daughters, and the couple had separated.

19:33 At the time of the arrest he was living with a daughter and a granddaughter. From 1990 until he retired in 2017, he had worked as a truck mechanic at a Save Mart distribution center in Roseville. His family had not suspected him. His eldest daughter called him a perfect father, and his wife had accepted his explanations for being away from home. Neighbors remembered loud, profane outbursts, and one family said he left them a phone message threatening to deliver a load of death because their dog barked.

20:00 His brother-in-law said that back in the seventies, DeAngelo would casually bring up the East Area Rapist in conversation. In July 2018, Sharon filed for divorce. He was charged that first day with eight counts of first-degree murder, and the announcement was the first time anyone publicly connected him to the Visalia Ransacker. Visalia's police chief said there was no DNA linking him to the Central Valley cases, but that other evidence made him confident the Ransacker had been caught.

20:25 In May, Santa Barbara County filed four more murder counts. In August came a murder charge for Claude Snelling and thirteen counts of kidnapping. The sexual assaults could not be charged at all, because California's statute of limitations on those cases from before 2017 had run out. Alone in an interview room after the arrest, according to a Sacramento County prosecutor, DeAngelo talked to himself. He said, I didn't have the strength to push him out. He made me. He went with me.

20:49 It was like in my head, I mean, he's a part of me. I didn't want to do those things. I pushed Jerry out and had a happy life. I did all those things. I destroyed all their lives. So now I've got to pay the price. Prosecutors believe it was a tactic, to manipulate the police or to lay the ground for an insanity defense. He had admitted to a similar act after the shoplifting arrest in 1979. Detectives had ignored his first requests for a lawyer, on the understanding that the interview could not be used against him in court.

21:16 His was the first public arrest made through genetic genealogy, and it set off a national argument. He never handed his DNA to anyone. His relatives did, for their own reasons, and that was enough. Some critics call government searches of those databases a breach of privacy. According to a study published in Science Magazine, about sixty percent of people of European descent in America, and about forty percent of people of Sub-Saharan African descent, can be identified through the MyHeritage database alone.

21:41 Since his arrest, more than a hundred and fifty suspects have been identified the same way. The prosecution was going to be enormous. In November 2018, prosecutors from six counties estimated the case could cost taxpayers twenty million dollars and last ten years. In April 2019 they announced they would seek the death penalty, and the judge ruled that cameras could be allowed in the courtroom during the trial. There was no trial. On June 29, 2020, in Sacramento, Joseph DeAngelo pleaded guilty to thirteen counts of first-degree murder and thirteen counts of kidnapping.

22:12 In exchange, the death penalty was taken off the table. The deal also required him to admit to crimes he had never been charged with, including the sexual assaults, and prosecutors described them in court in horrific detail. From August 18 to August 20, 2020, the people he hurt got their turn. Survivors, their relatives, and the families of the people he killed spoke to him in court. He sat in a wheelchair, his face covered by a white mask. Jane Carson-Sandler was attacked in 1976, at home with her young son.

22:40 She told the court that her attention that night was fully on her child. Where did you put my son when you removed him from the bed, she asked him. Where did you put him and what were you going to do to him? She said that decades later, a ski mask or someone yelling shut up will forever cause her anxiety. And she said her comfort at those times is remembering that he is finally going to prison and will remain there until he dies. When the statements were done, DeAngelo rose out of the wheelchair and spoke without the face covering.

23:07 I've listened to all your statements, he said. Each one of them. And I'm truly sorry to everyone I have hurt. Thank you, your honor. On August 21, 2020, Joseph James DeAngelo was sentenced to life in prison without the possibility of parole, more than twelve life sentences in all. No court will ever convict him of the attacks that gave the East Area Rapist his name. His own admission of them, made in open court, is what stands on the record instead. So what does the evidence actually show?

23:33 It shows a man who carried a badge through the first years of his crimes, who knew how police worked and how little their departments shared. It shows agencies that guarded their own cases and denied links that turned out to be real. And it shows evidence that outlasted every one of those failures, because someone in a Ventura medical examiner's office took the trouble to freeze it. It also shows what the long hunt cost along the way. David Witthuhn is not the only man who lived under suspicion.

23:57 Several others were looked at over the years and cleared, by DNA, by alibis, or by other investigative work. And a method that can find a killer through his distant cousins can find almost anyone that way. Since the arrest, people have kept raising questions about the ethics of using that kind of personal information. The file is closed on Joseph DeAngelo, yet the forensic precedent his capture established continues to reshape criminal investigations nationwide. Read the file.

24:23 Form your own conclusion. That's it for this one. Thanks for hanging out with me. This has been CrimeTimeSnacks, and I'll catch you next time.
