The Touch DNA of JonBenet

November 24, 2026  ·  Cory

Listen on Spotify Apple Podcasts

In 1996, six-year-old JonBenet Ramsey was found dead in the basement of her family's home in Boulder, Colorado. The initial police work was heavily criticized, and early suspicion fell on her parents. Years later, trace DNA found on her clothing pointed to an unidentified male, and in 2008 the district attorney sent the family a letter clearing them. The episode walks through the timeline of that morning, the grand jury that voted to indict the parents, and the DNA evidence that changed the official account of the case.

This transcript is the episode script as written and fact-checked, timed to the recording. Timestamps are approximate within a sentence.

0:05 What's up guys, welcome back to CrimeTimeSnacks. On December 26, 1996, a Boulder police officer stood in front of a basement door, paused for a moment, and walked away without opening it. The body of a six-year-old girl was later found behind that door. Her name was JonBenét Ramsey. The case is nearly thirty years old, and no one has ever been charged. In 2008, a district attorney sent the Ramsey family a letter saying DNA results had cleared them. So what did that DNA show, and what did it not show?

0:31 Let's unpack it. JonBenét Patricia Ramsey was born on August 6, 1990, in Atlanta, Georgia. Her given name was made by combining her father's first and middle names, John Bennett. Her middle name was her mother's first name. Her father, John Ramsey, was a businessman and the president of Access Graphics, a computer company that later became a subsidiary of Lockheed Martin. His first marriage ended in divorce in 1978. Patsy was his second wife. In 1991 he moved with Patsy and the family to Boulder, Colorado, where Access Graphics had its headquarters.

1:02 JonBenét was the younger of John and Patsy's two children. Her brother, Burke, was born in 1987. She also had three older half-siblings from her father's first marriage. During the 1996 to 1997 school year she was enrolled in kindergarten at High Peaks Elementary School in Boulder. Patsy entered her in child beauty pageants. JonBenét won titles including America's Royale Miss, Little Miss Colorado and National Tiny Miss Beauty. After the murder, the media reported on her role in those pageants and on what was described as Patsy's pageant mother behavior.

1:33 In the early hours of December 26, 1996, after a family Christmas, Patsy Ramsey walked down the stairs of the family's home at 755 15th Street. She reported finding a handwritten ransom note on the kitchen staircase. It was two and a half pages long. The note was addressed to John Ramsey. It demanded $118,000 for the safe return of his daughter. It warned him that he stood a 99 percent chance of killing his daughter if he tried to outsmart the people who wrote it. The note contained specific instructions against contacting police and friends.

2:02 Patsy telephoned the police at 5:52 a.m. Mountain Standard Time. She also called family and friends. Two police officers responded to the call and arrived at the home within three minutes. They conducted a cursory search of the house and did not find any sign of forced entry. The only people known to be in the house on the night JonBenét died were her immediate family. That was John, Patsy and Burke. Officer Rick French went to the basement. He came to a door that was secured by an additional wooden latch at the top of the door frame.

2:29 He paused in front of it for a moment. Then he walked away without opening it. French later explained why. He was looking for an exit route used by the kidnapper. The wooden latch was holding the door closed from inside the house. A kidnapper could not have used that door and then closed the latch from the inside. So he ruled it out as a possible exit. With JonBenét still missing, John made arrangements to pay the ransom. A forensics team was dispatched to the house. The team initially believed the child had been kidnapped, and her bedroom was the only room in the house that was cordoned off to prevent contamination of evidence.

2:59 No precautions were taken to prevent contamination in the rest of the house. Friends, victim advocates and the Ramsey family's minister arrived at the home to show support. Visitors picked up and cleaned surfaces in the kitchen, possibly destroying evidence. Boulder detective Linda Arndt arrived at about 8:00 a.m., in anticipation of further instructions from the kidnapper or kidnappers. There was never an attempt by anyone to claim the money. ABC News reported there was no further communication from the would-be kidnappers.

3:28 At 1:00 p.m., Detective Arndt asked John Ramsey and a family friend, Fleet White, to search the house to see if anything seemed out of place. They started their search in the basement. John opened the latched door that Officer French had overlooked. His daughter's body lay in one of the rooms. Her mouth was covered with duct tape. A nylon cord was found around her wrists and her neck. Her torso was covered by a white blanket. John picked up her body and rushed upstairs. When she was moved, the crime scene was further contaminated, and critical forensic evidence was disturbed for the returning forensics team.

3:58 Her body was found about seven hours after she was reported missing. JonBenét was buried on December 31 in Marietta, Georgia. She was interred beside her half-sister, Elizabeth Pasch Ramsey, who had died in a car crash nearly five years earlier at age 22. The autopsy revealed that JonBenét was killed by strangulation and a skull fracture. The official cause of death was asphyxia by strangulation associated with craniocerebral trauma. Her skull had an eight-and-a-half-inch fracture on the right side of her head.

4:24 The death was ruled a homicide. A garrote was tied around her neck. It was made from a length of nylon cord and the broken handle of a paintbrush, and it had apparently been used to strangle her. Part of the bristle end of that paintbrush was found in a tub containing Patsy's art supplies. The bottom third of it was never found, despite extensive searching of the house by the police in the days that followed. There was no evidence of conventional rape, although sexual assault could not be ruled out.

4:48 No semen was found. There was evidence of a vaginal injury, and the pathologist recorded that the area appeared to have been wiped with a cloth. Evidence also suggested that the paintbrush used in the garrote was used for sexual assault. The autopsy also noted a, quote, "vegetable or fruit material which may represent pineapple," which JonBenét had eaten a few hours before her death. Photographs taken at the home that day show a bowl of pineapple on the kitchen table with a spoon in it.

5:13 Neither John nor Patsy said they remembered putting the bowl on the table or feeding pineapple to JonBenét. Police reported that they found Patsy's fingerprints on the bowl. Two neighbors gave statements about that night. Scott Gibbons said that around midnight, looking from his kitchen window, he saw the upper kitchen of the Ramsey residence lit up with dimmed lights. Melody Stanton reported that she awoke shortly after midnight to the sound of a child's scream coming from the Ramsey residence.

5:38 Now the ransom note. John pointed out to the first police on the scene that $118,000 was nearly identical to his Christmas bonus of the prior year. That suggested someone with access to that information would be involved in the crime. Investigators considered employees at Access Graphics who might have known the amount of the bonus. They also considered the possibility that the demand was a reference to Psalm 118, and they spoke to religious sources about it. The note was unusually long.

6:02 The FBI told the police it was very unusual for such a note to be written at the crime scene. The note and a practice draft were both written with a pen and a notepad from the Ramsey home. The note appears to echo film dialogue. Ruthless People, Ransom, Escape from New York, Speed and Dirty Harry are all considered potential sources. The police believed the note was staged. It did not have any fingerprints on it except for Patsy's and those of the authorities who had handled it, and it included an unusual use of exclamation marks and initialisms.

6:31 Then there is the handwriting. A Colorado Bureau of Investigation report said there were, quote, "indications that the author of the ransom note is Patricia Ramsey." But the evidence fell short of a definitive conclusion. ABC News reported that after numerous handwriting comparisons, only one indicated that the samples showed any similarities to Patsy's writing. Michael Baden is a board-certified forensic pathologist who consulted with both sides of the case. He said he had never seen a note like it in his 60-year experience, and that he did not think it was written by an outside stranger.

7:00 A federal court ruled it highly unlikely that Patsy wrote the note, citing six certified handwriting experts. The court lamented that self-proclaimed experts without credentials were interfering in the case by accusing Patsy with no scientific basis. The Boulder police initially focused almost exclusively on John and Patsy. Investigators believed Patsy had written the note. Police theorized that the note and the condition of JonBenét's body had been staged by her parents to conceal responsibility for the killing.

7:26 Gregg McCrary, a retired FBI profiler, said that statistically, quote, "it is a 12-to-1 probability that it's a family member or a caregiver" who is involved in the homicide of a child. The police saw no evidence of a forced entry. They did see what they considered evidence of staging, such as the ransom note. Each of the Ramseys provided handwriting, blood and hair samples to the police. John and Patsy took part in a preliminary interview for more than two hours. Burke was interviewed within the first couple of weeks after his sister's death.

7:54 The police did not find the Ramseys cooperative. According to the Daily Camera, the Ramseys said their reluctance came from a fear that there would not be a full investigation for intruders, and that they would be hastily selected as the key suspects. The Ramseys offered a $100,000 reward in a newspaper ad dated April 27, 1997. Three days later, more than four months after their daughter's body was found, they submitted for the first time to separate formal interviews at the Boulder County Justice Center.

8:20 Errors made in the initial investigation complicated its resolution. Those errors included loss and contamination of evidence, a lack of experienced and technical staff, evidence shared with the Ramseys, and delayed informal interviews with the parents. There was also this. Boulder Police intentionally leaked misleading information, in hopes of creating public pressure on John and Patsy to better cooperate with police interview requests. That contributed to the intense media attention surrounding the family.

8:48 By October 1997, the police had more than 1,600 people in their index of persons of interest for the case. Burke was nine years old when his sister died. Investigators interviewed him at least three times, and the interviews did not raise any concerns. A review by a child psychologist stated that it appeared the Ramseys had, quote, "healthy, caring family relationships." In 1998, Boulder Police Chief Mark Beckner said in an interview with a news reporter that Burke was not involved in the killing of his sister.

9:15 In May 1999, the Boulder County District Attorney's office reiterated that Burke was not a suspect. The investigators had never considered him one. Lou Smit was a detective who came out of retirement in early 1997 to assist the Boulder County District Attorney's office with the case. He assessed the evidence and concluded that an intruder had committed the crime. On the night JonBenét was killed, two windows had been left slightly open so that electrical cords for the outside Christmas lights could pass through.

9:42 There was a broken basement window. And there was one unlocked door. Smit's theory was that someone entered the home through the broken basement window. He believed the intruder subdued JonBenét using a stun gun and took her down to the basement. Critics have questioned that theory. There was an intact cobweb in the basement window. The steel grate that covered the window also had undisturbed cobwebs, and the foliage around the grate had been undisturbed. The police and the prosecutors followed leads for intruders partly because of an unidentified boot mark left in the basement room where JonBenét's body was found.

10:13 It was also determined that there had been more than 100 burglaries in the Ramseys' neighborhood in the months before the murder, and that 38 registered sex offenders were living within two miles of the home. In May 1998, Smit and other staff members of the district attorney's office presented his findings to the Boulder police, concluding that the evidence pointed away from the Ramseys. They were unable to successfully challenge the police department's belief that the Ramseys were guilty.

10:37 Smit resigned from the investigation on September 20, 1998. In his resignation letter he wrote that the case told him, quote, "that John and Patsy Ramsey did not kill their daughter, that a very dangerous killer is still out there and no one is actively looking for him." Another lead investigator, Steve Thomas, also resigned, and his view was the opposite. He believed the district attorney's office had interfered with and failed to support his hypothesis of the case. Smit was no longer an official investigator, but he continued to work on the case until his death in 2010.

11:08 Because of the animosity between the police and the district attorney's office, and the pressure to obtain a conviction, Colorado governor Roy Romer interceded. He named Michael Kane as special prosecutor to initiate a grand jury. The grand jury was convened beginning September 15, 1998. Smit resigned five days later. Among the experts who testified were DNA specialist Barry Scheck and forensic expert Henry Lee. In 1999, the grand jury voted to indict John and Patsy Ramsey for child abuse resulting in death and for accessory to a crime.

11:39 The indictment cited two counts each of child abuse. It said the parents, quote, "did unlawfully, knowingly, recklessly and feloniously permit a child to be unreasonably placed in a situation that posed a threat of injury to the child's life or health." A grand jury applies a probable cause standard. A criminal conviction requires proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. On October 13, 1999, District Attorney Alex Hunter refused to sign the indictment, saying the evidence was insufficient for prosecution.

12:08 He did not believe he could meet that higher standard, and he did not prosecute them. That same year, Colorado Governor Bill Owens spoke out. He told the Ramseys to, quote, "quit hiding behind their attorneys, quit hiding behind their PR firm." The public thought the grand jury investigation had been inconclusive. In 2002, the statute of limitations on the grand jury's charges expired. The vote to indict was not revealed publicly until October 25, 2013, when previously sealed court documents were released.

12:36 Now the DNA. On January 15, 1997, just weeks after the girl's death, DNA lab results were sent from the Colorado Bureau of Investigation to the lead detective, Thomas Trujillo. Fox News Digital reported on those documents. They show that DNA evidence recovered under JonBenét's fingernails and from her clothes was not a match for members of the family and others close to the case. The report said that if the minor components from three exhibits were contributed by a single individual, then nine named people, among them John, Patricia and Burke Ramsey, quote, "would be excluded as a source of the DNA analyzed on those exhibits."

13:10 John Ramsey has said he believes an outside private lab, Bode Labs in Virginia, did the first evidence testing, and that it reported in January 1997 that it found unidentified male DNA in JonBenét's underwear. In December 2003, forensic investigators extracted enough material from a mixed blood sample found on her underwear to establish a DNA profile. That DNA belonged to an unknown male. It excluded each of the Ramseys. The profile was submitted to CODIS, the FBI's Combined DNA Index System, a database containing more than 1.6 million DNA profiles.

13:42 It did not match any profile in the database. Hundreds of DNA tests were performed to find a match to the DNA recovered during her autopsy. By then the case had changed hands. Mary Lacy, the next Boulder County District Attorney, took over the investigation from the police on December 26, 2002. She advanced the theory that an intruder had entered the home and committed the killing. In April 2003, Lacy agreed with a federal judge who sat on a libel case that the evidence was, quote, "more consistent with a theory that an intruder murdered JonBenét than it was with a theory that Mrs. Ramsey did."

14:16 In a defamation lawsuit called Wolf versus Ramsey, Judge Julie Carnes wrote that there was virtually no evidence to support the theory that the Ramseys murdered their child, but abundant evidence to support their belief that an intruder entered their home during the night of December 25, 1996, and killed their daughter. On August 15, 2006, a 41-year-old school teacher named John Mark Karr was arrested in Bangkok, Thailand, following a false confession to the murder. He had provided only basic facts that were publicly known.

14:43 He claimed JonBenét had been drugged, and the autopsy indicated no drugs were found in her body. Karr's DNA did not match the DNA found on her body. Patsy Ramsey died of ovarian cancer in 2006, at age 49. She is interred next to her daughter. Two years after Patsy's death came the test this episode is named for. In 2008, DNA was detected on the outside of JonBenét's pajama bottoms using a new testing method called touch DNA analysis. It matched the DNA found inside her underwear.

15:11 It traced to the same unknown male, and not to any member of the Ramsey family. On July 9, 2008, the Boulder District Attorney's office announced that, as a result of the newly developed techniques, the Ramsey family members were excluded as suspects in the case. Mary Lacy wrote a letter to John Ramsey. It said, quote, "This new scientific evidence convinces us that it is appropriate, given the circumstances of this case, to state that we do not consider your immediate family, including you, your wife, Patsy, and your son, Burke, to be under any suspicion in the commission of this crime."

15:42 She added, "I wish we could have done so before Mrs. Ramsey died." On February 2, 2009, Police Chief Mark Beckner announced that Stan Garnett, the new Boulder County District Attorney, was turning the case over to the police, and that his team would resume investigating it. Garnett found that the statute of limitations for the crimes identified in the 1999 grand jury true bill had expired, and he did not pursue review of the case against the Ramseys. In 2016, he said that Lacy's decision was not legally binding, and that Lacy never should have written the letter.

16:14 ABC News reported that some Boulder officials said the letter was inappropriate for a prosecutor, and that she should not have cleared the Ramseys while the investigation was ongoing. Steven Pitt, a forensic psychiatrist hired by Boulder authorities, said, quote, "Lacy's public exoneration of the Ramseys was a big slap in the face to Chief Beckner and the core group of detectives who had been working on the case for years." So what does that DNA prove? The experts quoted on this case do not agree.

16:39 Former FBI profiler Candice Delong believes the DNA belongs to the killer, because it showed up identically in several different places on multiple surfaces. She has said, quote, "This is a DNA case." Bob Grant is a former Adams County District Attorney who assisted the Boulder district attorney's office on the case for many years. He also believes the DNA evidence is significant. He said any resolution of the case would have to explain how the DNA showed up on several pieces of JonBenét's clothing.

17:06 Lawrence Kobilinsky, a DNA and forensic science expert, appeared in a documentary called The Killing of JonBenét: The Truth Uncovered, broadcast on September 5, 2016. He stated that, based on forensic DNA analysis of evidence, quote, "an unidentified male committed this crime." Michael Baden reads it differently. He said, quote, "Trace amounts of DNA can get on places and clothing from all different, nonsuspicious means. There is no forensic evidence to show that this is a stranger murder."

17:33 Baden noted an old study from Dr. Henry Lee. It found that trace DNA could be left on underwear by a number of random people who had handled it, ranging from factory workers to laundry workers. On the 1997 lab report, Baden said the document contains no smoking gun. He said, quote, "The DNA could've been on fingernails and on the panties before she was attacked, not related to the perpetrator." Delong does not accept the factory explanation. In an interview sampled on the Dr. Phil show, she laughingly dismissed the idea that the same touch DNA could have shown up on multiple items of JonBenét's clothing from the same factory worker.

18:07 There is also the question of how many people the DNA came from. In October 2016, a report said that new forensic analysis with more sensitive techniques revealed that the original DNA contained genetic markers from two individuals other than JonBenét. James Kolar, who was a lead investigator for the district attorney's office, said there were additional traces of male DNA found on the cord and the paintbrush that Mary Lacy did not mention. He said the testing found six separate DNA samples belonging to unknown individuals.

18:34 Gordon Coombes, a former investigator for the Boulder County District Attorney's office, questioned total absolution of the Ramseys in 2016. He said, quote, "We all shed DNA all the time within our skin cells. It can be deposited anywhere at any time for various reasons, reasons that are benign." He said clearing somebody just on the premise of touch DNA, especially when the crime scene wasn't secure at the beginning, quote, "really is a stretch." Then there is Mark Beckner. In 2015 he disagreed with exonerating the Ramseys.

19:02 He said, quote, "Exonerating anyone based on a small piece of evidence that has not yet been proved to even be connected to the crime is absurd." But Beckner also said the unknown DNA from JonBenét's clothing, quote, "has got to be the focus of the investigation." And he said that until one can prove otherwise, "the suspect is the donator of that unknown DNA." Put those statements side by side. The DNA has got to be the focus of the investigation. And it has not yet been proved to be connected to the crime.

19:29 Both of those come from the same police chief. John Douglas is a former FBI special agent and criminal profiler who was hired by the Ramsey family. He contends that John and Patsy did not kill their daughter. He said, quote, "Many crimes are tried in the court of public opinion long before they reach a court of law. But I know of no other case in which the majority of people have decided the solution based on statistics." L. Lin Wood, the Ramsey family's libel attorney, filed defamation lawsuits starting in 1999 against several people and companies that had reported on the case.

19:58 The family sued Star magazine and its parent company, American Media, on their son's behalf that year. On September 18 and 19, 2016, CBS broadcast a show called The Case of: JonBenét Ramsey. Its group of experts theorized that Burke hit his sister in the head with a heavy object, possibly a flashlight, perhaps not intending to kill her. Investigators had never considered Burke a suspect. The show's team also examined the DNA. They concluded that the trace amount found on the underwear could have been transferred when the underwear was made and packaged.

20:29 A review in Variety questioned the objectivity of the team. Rolling Stone magazine found three ways in which the show's investigation was flawed. One of them was, quote, "Dismissing the DNA evidence entirely." Forensic pathologist Werner Spitz accused Burke Ramsey of killing his sister, in the show and in a September 2016 interview with CBS Detroit. On October 6, 2016, Burke filed a defamation lawsuit against Spitz. He and his attorneys sought a total of 150 million dollars in punitive and compensatory damages.

20:59 On December 28, 2016, Burke's lawyers filed an additional civil lawsuit against CBS, the production company Critical Content, and seven experts and consultants. They sought 250 million dollars in compensatory damages and 500 million dollars in punitive damages. In January 2018, a judge denied the CBS motion to dismiss. In January 2019, Wood announced that the lawsuit had been settled, quote, "to the satisfaction of all parties." Burke Ramsey later said this. Quote, "We didn't get spanked, nothing of the sort, nothing close, nothing near laying a finger on us, let alone killing your child."

21:34 In October 2010, the Boulder police reopened the cold case. New interviews were conducted following a fresh inquiry by a committee that included state and federal investigators. There was no new information gleaned from those interviews. The Boulder Police Department continues to treat the case as an open homicide. On June 23, 2026, a former Colorado Bureau of Investigation forensic scientist named Yvonne Woods pleaded guilty to four felonies in connection with a DNA testing scandal.

22:00 According to prosecutors, she altered and deleted data related to critical portions of the laboratory's DNA quality control process. She worked for the agency from 1994 until 2023. The Boulder Police Department said that, after a review of all the records by the Colorado Bureau of Investigation, it does not believe her actions had any effect on the JonBenét Ramsey case. The district attorney's office said the review determined her conduct will not have an impact on the evidence.

22:27 ABC News describes a new technology that can retrieve DNA from very small samples. Combined with genetic genealogy, it allows officials to identify suspects by matching DNA to that of distant family members. John Ramsey wants that used on his daughter's case. He told Fox News Digital that the family has the killer's DNA, and that forensic genetic genealogy is, quote, "the new tool which could give us the killer's name if the police would only use it." Ramsey said the work should be done by one of the specialized private laboratories with expertise in forensic genetic genealogy.

22:57 He said, quote, "We have been begging the police to work with one of these labs, but we don't know if they will do it." The district attorney's office said authorities are actively exploring advanced DNA testing capabilities through outside independent laboratories. ABC also reported the difficulty. Some DNA samples obtained from the body and the crime scene are very small and complex, with multiple DNA strands mixed together. That makes it difficult to verifiably match them with a single individual.

23:23 The current Boulder Police Chief, Stephen Redfearn, had never before done an interview about the Ramsey case. He sat down with ABC News anchor Diane Sawyer and told her that his department is in talks with labs that might be able to test the DNA using forensic genetic genealogy. Redfearn said they are hesitant to send any sample until they have full confidence that the technology is advanced enough to get results. If a sample is very small, it can be consumed by the testing. He said, quote, "We might get lucky because we may only, in some circumstances, with what we have, have one shot at getting it tested."

23:54 Redfearn said they are re-interviewing suspects, testing new evidence and pursuing every lead. He said, quote, "I would love nothing more than 30 years since this little girl was murdered in her own home, someone finally decides to get something off their chest." After Lou Smit's death, his family shared thousands of his documents with a group that calls itself the JonBenét Ramsey Smit Family Team. John Anderson, a former El Paso County sheriff and a member of that team, said they started a GoFundMe account with a target of $100,000 and had raised over $95,000.

24:23 He said they have offered to use it to pay for the genetic testing. JonBenét's older half-brother, John Andrew Ramsey, told ABC, quote, "This is a case that can be solved. There's a mountain of evidence out there. We've got the DNA testing that's in our favor. It's not a question of if, but when." Burke Ramsey told ABC, quote, "This awful thing happened to this family, and the world turns against you. And it's cruel." So here is where the case file stands. An unknown male DNA profile was established in 2003, and in 2008 the DNA on her pajama bottoms matched it.

24:54 Mary Lacy wrote the family a letter clearing them, and Stan Garnett, the district attorney after her, said Lacy never should have written it. A grand jury voted to indict, and the district attorney refused to sign. No one has ever been charged. Bob Grant said this about the case. Quote, "The case will always be, in my mind, one where there are two likely scenarios. And to prove one, you have to disprove the other." Read the file. Form your own conclusion. That's it for this one.
